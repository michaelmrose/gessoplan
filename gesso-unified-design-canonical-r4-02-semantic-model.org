* Part II — Semantic model
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: part-02-semantic-model
:END:

This part defines the semantic distinctions shared by choreography, authoritative
operations, browser realization, and application assembly. It uses the assurance
and admission vocabulary established in Part I. The definitions do not require
a universal persisted fact graph, a new runtime class for every concept, or a
single global revision coordinate.

** Values and evolving values

A value may be a singular immutable value:

#+begin_src clojure
42
:open
{:user/id user-id}
#+end_src

A value may also be understood as evolving across a time, revision, causal, or
transformation-step dimension.

For example:

#+begin_example
foo = :bar at T1
foo = :baz at T2
#+end_example

or:

#+begin_example
foo = :bar at step S1
foo = :baz at step S2
#+end_example

The observations themselves may remain immutable facts.

The evolving value is the relationship among those observations and the rules
used to select or interpret one observation relative to a particular point of
view.

"Current value" is therefore not an eternal mutable fact.

It is a derived view relative to some combination of:

- valid/effective time;
- causal or transformation relation;
- authoritative basis;
- authoritative progression;
- actor knowledge frontier;
- application policy.

For a HumanHelp Request:

#+begin_example
F1: Request R was unclaimed at valid time T1.
F2: Request R was claimed by user U at valid time T2.
#+end_example

Both statements can remain true under the model's declared valid-time and
correction semantics. The historical fact that an observation was made is
distinct from a claim that its interpretation of the world can never be
corrected. Recording an earlier observation does not make an erroneous domain
assertion eternally true.

The Request's current state depends on the point from which the Request is
evaluated and on later facts that supersede, refine, reconcile, or otherwise
relate to the earlier history.

The framework must not confuse:

#+begin_example
fact existed
fact became authoritative
fact became visible at a database frontier
actor learned fact
actor derived another fact
actor displayed representation of fact
#+end_example

These are distinct events and relationships.

*** Conceptual fact graph versus required persistence

The semantic model may reason in terms of facts and relations among facts
without requiring Gesso to persist a universal fact DAG.

The conceptual graph is useful because it allows the design to reason about:

- provenance;
- supersession;
- derivation;
- authority;
- agreement;
- causality;
- command/result relations;
- provisional versus authoritative state.

Actual persistence remains owned by application models and their chosen
authoritative systems.

For HumanHelp, XTDB and public model semantics remain the primary persistence
and authority boundary.

Gesso must not introduce a duplicate universal fact store merely because the
proof vocabulary can describe facts.

*** Immutability and correction

The statement that observations are immutable does not prohibit an application
from correcting its model of the world.

A later authoritative fact may state that an earlier interpretation was wrong,
superseded, withdrawn, or effective over a different valid-time interval.

What must remain explicit is the relationship.

The system should avoid interpreting absence from a current projection as
evidence that an earlier observation never occurred. This semantic distinction
does not require indefinite storage of every observation, prohibit legitimate
data deletion, or introduce an audit-history retention policy. Persistence and
retention remain model-owned.

This distinction becomes important for:

- reconciliation;
- idempotency;
- audit;
- stale-command interpretation;
- provisional settlement;
- authoritative reread;
- domain-specific corrections.

*** Semantic value versus representation

A semantic value is not identical to a wire encoding, DOM representation, XTDB
object, or Clojure map merely because one of those represents it.

Representations may change while semantic identity remains stable.

Conversely, two values with identical serialized shape may have different
semantic meaning because their:

- provenance differs;
- authority differs;
- basis differs;
- principal differs;
- operation context differs;
- compatibility generation differs.

Shape equality alone is insufficient when the semantic judgment depends on
distinctions not established by that shape. Ordinary immutable values may still
use ordinary value equality when the enclosing contract makes it sufficient.
Gesso must not mistake matching schemas or encodings for equality of authority,
provenance, subject, observation scope, or admission status.

*** Representation, semantic category, and admission
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-semantic-admission
:END:

Representation validity, semantic category, semantic admission, and proof are
separate questions.

A Clojure map, record, scalar, wire value, DOM node, or other representation may
carry a semantic value without the representation itself establishing the
stronger status in which later code intends to use it.

The design therefore distinguishes conceptually:

#+begin_example
representation has the required shape
        !=
value belongs to the required semantic category
        !=
value has crossed the admission/checking boundary for that category
        !=
the strongest propositions associated with that value have been proved
#+end_example

This distinction matters most where two values may have the same or nearly the
same representation while differing materially in provenance, authority,
lifecycle, or assurance status.  Examples include:

- =ObservedBasisClaim= versus an authoritative basis established by trusted
  acquisition;
- =CommandId= versus =ExecutionId=;
- provisional versus authoritative projections;
- raw versus checked choreography declarations;
- projected plans versus admitted executable plans;
- raw certificate data versus checker-accepted certificate evidence;
- authenticated observation evidence versus an operation capability;
- historical observation evidence versus current authorization.

The supported Gesso APIs must not silently treat one such category as another
merely because the underlying values have equal map shape, equal scalar
representation, matching discriminator keys, or compatible Malli schemas.

This is an admission rule rather than a requirement to turn every semantic
concept into a new runtime class.  Permitted realizations include, where they
fit the existing implementation:

- nominal types or records;
- opaque wrappers;
- smart or checked constructors;
- values reachable only through a verifier, elaborator, authoritative
  acquisition path, or registry lookup;
- tagged representations whose tags are validated by the appropriate trusted
  boundary;
- existing Gesso representations that already make the distinction impossible
  or immediately rejectable.

The design requires the semantic distinction and its enforcement at the
supported boundary.  It does not prescribe a nominal-type rewrite when an
existing mechanism already provides the required protection.

Nominality is especially useful as misuse prevention inside cooperating trusted
application code.  For example, if =CommandId= and =ExecutionId= are both
physically UUIDs, a supported API should not make accidental interchange look
ordinary merely because both values satisfy a UUID schema.  Similarly, a raw
map containing the fields of a checked certificate must not become accepted
certificate evidence solely by carrying a discriminator such as
=:certificate/type= or =:verified? true=.

A boundary must retain or establish the information required to distinguish
the categories it promises to check. If two untagged UUIDs reach a boundary
with no trustworthy category or correlation information, UUID shape validation
cannot reconstruct whether one originated as a command or an execution.

The supported construction path must therefore preserve the needed distinction
through checked construction, typed or opaque values, declared field bindings,
trusted correlation, or another mechanism that actually enforces the contract.
Merely assigning different variable names to interchangeable unchecked inputs
does not establish that protection. Framework-owned construction should derive
these bindings wherever it already possesses the required information.

Nominal or opaque representation alone is not a security proof.  A value called
=VerifiedChoreography= is trustworthy only to the extent that its construction
or admission path actually establishes the claims downstream code relies on.
Likewise, a Clojure record constructor is not treated as a cryptographic or
process-isolation boundary.

Crossing an untrusted serialization boundary also strips any purely in-process
admission status.  Conceptually:

#+begin_example
server-side admitted value
        |
        | encode / transmit
        v
untrusted bytes or client data
        |
        | decode + verify / re-establish trusted premises
        v
server-side admitted value, if justified
#+end_example

Deserializing data into the same nominal-looking representation must not by
itself restore authority, trusted provenance, certification, or admission.

Ordinary downstream application authoring should not pay unnecessary ceremony
for these distinctions.  Gesso should preferentially construct admitted values
behind verification, elaboration, acquisition, projection, model, and assembly
boundaries.  Routine application code should not be required to manually wrap
every Request id, title, status, or other ordinary domain value merely to
satisfy framework ceremony.

The practical acceptance criterion is:

#+begin_quote
Where two same-shaped values have materially different semantic meanings,
accidental substitution through the supported API is either structurally
unavailable or rejected at the earliest legitimate boundary with a diagnostic
that identifies the expected semantic category, the supplied category, and the
missing derivation or admission step. If the supplied category cannot be
established, the diagnostic identifies it as unknown or unadmitted rather than
inventing a trustworthy origin from an untrusted tag.
#+end_quote

** Facts, time, and authoritative progression

The design distinguishes at least the following dimensions.

*** Valid/effective time

Valid or effective time describes when a domain fact applies in the modeled
world.

Examples:

- Membership became active on one date;
- a Request was open during one interval;
- Organization policy changed at a domain-effective time;
- a correction applies retroactively to an earlier period.

Valid time belongs to domain meaning.

*** Domain or causal relation

Domain or causal relations describe how semantic facts relate.

Examples include:

- based on;
- produced by;
- superseded by;
- reconciled by;
- rejected by;
- confirmed by;
- corrected by;
- derived from.

These relations are not necessarily database transaction order.

*** Authoritative database progression

Authoritative database progression describes the consistency/basis frontier at
which authoritative facts are visible.

For HumanHelp's primary persistent authority, this is supplied through XTDB
semantics.

An authoritative progression value may advance because unrelated data changed.

Therefore:

#+begin_example
database advanced
!=
this Request changed
#+end_example

*** Actor knowledge frontier

An actor knowledge frontier describes what authoritative information a
particular runtime actor has acquired through its admitted observation paths.
It is scoped by the facts, authority, projection, and relevant temporal context
of those observations. It need not be one token covering all actor knowledge.

For the supported browser implementation, the frontier records semantic
acquisition and incorporation by that implementation. It is not a server proof
that an adversarial browser or the human using it retained or understood the
information.

Two browser actors may observe the same authoritative database at different
frontiers.

The same principal may therefore have two browser tabs with different
knowledge.

The database's current state and an actor's current knowledge are distinct.

*** Physical delivery time

Physical delivery time may matter operationally without becoming semantic
authority.

An SSE notification may arrive after another message.

A callback may fire after an execution has been retired.

A browser may wake after a deployment.

The fact that an event arrived later in wall-clock time does not by itself make
the event semantically newer or authorized to mutate current state.

*** Do not collapse dimensions into one revision integer

The framework must not collapse:

#+begin_example
valid time
domain fact identity/version
causal relation
XTDB progression
actor knowledge frontier
command identity
execution identity
artifact compatibility identity
#+end_example

into one global monotonically increasing application revision.

A global revision number would erase distinctions that matter to correctness.

For example, XTDB may advance while Request R is unchanged.

Likewise, Request R may have meaningful valid-time history independent of when a
database transaction was recorded.

A browser may hold knowledge through frontier X17 while authority has progressed
to X23 without any relevant Request fact changing.

That is ordinary distributed execution, not necessarily staleness requiring
rejection.

*** Progression as provenance

An observed authoritative basis records something approximately like:

#+begin_quote
The representation from which this actor formed its command was justified by an
authoritative read consistent through this frontier.
#+end_quote

It does not mean:

- the entire database is still at that frontier;
- every fact the command depends on is unchanged;
- the client owns authority over that frontier;
- the client can prove merely by sending the token that it really performed the
  observation;
- the framework should reject every command formed from an older frontier.

The authoritative-operations and progression part defines the exact command,
scope, and XTDB semantics, including
[[#req-progression-scope][scoped progression requirements]].

At the ontology level, the important point is that authoritative progression is
one provenance coordinate among several and is not a universal domain revision.

** Roles, principals, actors, hosts, and authorities
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-semantic-identities
:END:

These concepts are distinct and must remain distinct throughout declaration,
projection, runtime execution, proof, diagnostics, and application assembly.

*** Role

A *role* is a static position in a choreography.

Examples:

#+begin_example
:helper
:request-authority
:observer
#+end_example

A role describes a position in the distributed protocol.

A role is not automatically:

- a user;
- a browser tab;
- a process;
- a web server;
- an authorization grant.

A principal or actor may instantiate a role only under the rules of the
particular operation and trusted runtime boundary.

*** Principal

A *principal* is the identity or entity whose authority is being exercised.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:user user-id]
[:service :humanhelp]
#+end_example

A principal is a semantic/security identity.

A principal is not identical to a browser actor.

One principal may have:

- multiple browser tabs;
- multiple devices;
- multiple protocol executions;
- retries through multiple hosts.

Those actors may hold different knowledge while acting for the same principal.

*** Actor

An *actor* is a concrete participant carrying a local projected machine,
execution state, or knowledge state.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:client client-id]
[:service-instance logical-service-actor-id]
#+end_example

An actor gives semantic identity to a participant beyond one physical callback
or request.

The exact runtime realization may be short-lived.

An actor identity does not require a permanently resident process.

*** Host

A *host* is the physical runtime that happens to execute a step.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:web-node node-1]
[:web-node node-3]
#+end_example

Hosts are replaceable physical realizations.

A retry may run on another host without changing:

- semantic command;
- principal;
- authority;
- logical operation;
- relevant actor identity, where the protocol preserves it.

A host does not acquire semantic authority merely because it executes authority
code.

#+LATEX: \newpage

*** Authority

An *authority* is the locus entitled to establish some class of authoritative
fact.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:authority :request]
[:authority :request request-id]
#+end_example

Authority is semantic.

A physical web node may temporarily realize an authoritative operation without
becoming the authority itself.

For a Request operation:

#+begin_example
semantic authority
    [:authority :request R]

first host
    [:web-node A]

retry host
    [:web-node C]
#+end_example

The authority survives host replacement.

*** Server is not a universal semantic identity

"Server" is normally an implementation location or a role category, not one
unique actor identity.

Multiple web nodes may realize the same logical authority while holding
different process-local state.

The design therefore avoids proofs or runtime assumptions that rely on:

#+begin_example
same server process
= same semantic authority
#+end_example

or:

#+begin_example
different server process
= different semantic authority
#+end_example

Neither equivalence is generally valid.

*** Relationship among role, principal, actor, host, and authority

A useful conceptual example is:

#+begin_example
role
    :helper

principal
    [:user U]

actor
    [:client C]

host of the browser-side actor
    user's browser/device

authoritative role
    :request-authority

semantic authority
    [:authority :request R]

host currently realizing authority
    [:web-node A]
#+end_example

A later retry may change the final line while leaving the semantic operation
otherwise intact.

Verification and diagnostics should identify which category an identity belongs
to instead of overloading one generic "id" field wherever practical.

Authentication establishes the principal binding. The operation's trusted
admission and model policy establish whether that principal may instantiate a
particular role for a particular subject. Actor and host identity provide
correlation or realization information; neither substitutes for those decisions.
Changing a browser tab, transport connection, or physical host must not silently
carry permissions into a different principal or authority scope.

** Logical identity and physical realization
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-logical-physical-identity
:END:

Logical identity must not be inferred from incidental physical identity.

The following identities are distinct:

#+begin_example
role identity
principal identity
actor identity
authority identity

semantic operation identity
semantic command identity
protocol execution identity

global choreography identity
projected-plan compatibility identity
exact executable artifact identity

logical fragment/target identity
request generation
effect/resource generation
continuity-slot generation

physical request identity
physical DOM-node identity
web-node/host identity
#+end_example

Some applications may not need every category in every operation.

The architecture must nevertheless avoid conflating categories merely because
one implementation currently uses the same value for two of them.

*** Semantic command identity

A semantic command identifies an attempted semantic operation.

It may survive:

- network retry;
- host replacement;
- a new protocol execution;
- another transport attempt;

where operation semantics permit that retry.

The [[#req-command-participant-uncertainty][command identity and uncertainty contract]]
in the authoritative-operations part defines the relevant retry and attribution
conditions. Possessing the same command identifier alone does not establish
that a retry is permitted or that the command already committed.

*** Protocol execution identity

A protocol execution identifies one realization of a distributed trajectory.

Two executions may relate to the same semantic command without being the same
execution.

Their resources and callbacks must not be interchangeable merely because the
semantic command matches.

*** Plan compatibility identity

A projected plan may remain in a browser across deployment.

Compatibility identity answers whether that suspended or retried projection is
safe to continue under the current server/runtime contract.

Compatibility does not necessarily require exact artifact equality. It must
nevertheless follow an explicit supported compatibility rule. A runtime must
not infer compatibility from matching names, approximate version ordering, or
the absence of a visible error.

*** Exact executable artifact identity

Diagnostic and proof correspondence may require a stronger identity than
runtime compatibility.

The exact executable artifact identity answers:

#+begin_quote
Does this diagnostic or proof metadata describe these exact emitted runtime
coordinates and transitions?
#+end_quote

A compatibility generation and an exact executable digest therefore serve
different purposes. Evidence tied to exact emitted coordinates must not be
attached to a merely compatible artifact as though exact correspondence held.
Conversely, a compatible continuation need not be rejected solely because
diagnostic-only material changed, if the declared compatibility contract permits
it and any exact correspondence claim remains accurate.

*** Logical target identity

A logical UI target may survive replacement of its physical DOM node.

For example, a Request card may remain the same logical fragment after HTMX
replaces the element representing it.

The runtime must not use physical-node identity as the sole semantic target
identity when representation replacement is expected.
** Resource instance identity
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-resource-instance-identity
:END:

A replaceable physical or runtime resource must be distinguishable from prior
instances of the same logical resource when events from those prior instances
could still arrive.

For example:

#+begin_example
logical slot S
    |
resource instance R1

replace R1
    |
resource instance R2

late callback from R1
    |
    X  must not mutate state belonging to R2
#+end_example

Resource instance identity answers a narrow question:

#+begin_quote
Does this event belong to the currently admitted instance of this replaceable
resource?
#+end_quote

A generation number, generation counter, epoch, opaque token, UUID, object
identity, or another suitable mechanism may represent resource instance
identity. The representation must provide the properties required by the boundary;
additional ordering or structure is unnecessary unless the contract uses it.

A generation counter is appropriate when monotonic ordering between successive
instances is useful. A distinct opaque identity is sufficient when the only
required question is whether an event belongs to the current instance.

The distinction must remain valid for as long as callbacks from an earlier
instance can still reach the boundary. Reusing a token, resetting a counter, or
wrapping a generation value must not make a retired instance indistinguishable
from its replacement. A reset is safe only when a separate scope or lifecycle
boundary prevents old events from being admitted as current.

Resource instance identity must not be used as a compressed representation of
independent semantic facts.

An event may be inadmissible because it belongs to a retired resource instance,
but it may also be inadmissible because its command, execution, plan, artifact,
authoritative basis, ownership, principal, authorization context, or another
required semantic correspondence no longer matches the current operation.

Those distinctions remain separate even when several of them happen to change
at the same time.

For example:

#+begin_example
continuity slot S
    |
resource instance R7
    |
execution X12
    |
authoritative basis B40

physical resource replaced

continuity slot S
    |
resource instance R8
    |
execution X12
    |
authoritative basis B40

late callback carrying R7
    |
    X  rejected because the resource instance is stale
#+end_example

In this case the execution and authoritative basis may still correspond. The
callback is rejected specifically because it belongs to the replaced physical
resource instance.

Conversely:

#+begin_example
continuity slot S
    |
resource instance R8
    |
execution X12
    |
authoritative basis B40

authoritative state advances

continuity slot S
    |
resource instance R8
    |
execution X12
    |
authoritative basis B41
#+end_example

The resource instance has not changed, but an operation that requires basis B41
must not treat possession of R8 as evidence that B40 remains admissible.

This separation prevents one generation counter, epoch, or opaque identity from
becoming a universal proxy for semantic freshness.

** Required correspondence checks are semantic gates
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-owner-relative-event-admission
:END:

At every runtime boundary, an event must satisfy the particular identity and
correspondence conditions required by that boundary.

Depending on the operation, those conditions may include:

- current resource instance identity;
- command identity;
- execution identity;
- plan or artifact identity;
- authoritative basis;
- ownership;
- authenticated principal;
- current authorization;
- continuity or provisional ownership state.

A boundary need not check every category. It must check the categories on which
its correctness actually depends.

These checks are not merely debug assertions. They determine whether a runtime
event is semantically admissible.

The design therefore does not require one universal generation value. It
requires independent semantic distinctions to remain independently
representable and requires each runtime event to satisfy the correspondence
conditions appropriate to the operation it is attempting to affect.

*** Admission follows the declared semantic owner

Every asynchronous event capable of influencing Gesso semantic state passes
the common semantic admission boundary. The boundary first resolves the event's
declared class and semantic owner, then applies the correspondences required
by that class.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
normalized event
    -> resolve declared event class and owner
    -> validate owner-specific correlation and applicability
    -> validate the permitted transition
    -> admit transition or classify rejection/recovery
#+end_example

The owner is not universally a command execution.

| Event class | Relevant owner and checks |
|-------------+---------------------------|
| Execution-private callback | Admitted execution, current resource instance, ownership, correlation, and permitted execution state |
| Shared fragment advisory | Declared subscription or fragment coordination scope, admitted source/correlation, current source instance where required, and valid advisory requirement |
| Authoritative acquisition completion | Admitted acquisition owner, request/resource correlation, returned observation scope and progression, and applicable installation rules |
| Continuity completion | Declared continuity owner or slot, current resource instance, and restoration rights still held for the target |

This table identifies distinctions rather than prescribing one public event
record or a closed enumeration of all future events. Each supported event class
must define its own required correspondence contract.

An idle board can therefore admit a legitimate shared advisory without an
unrelated command execution. A shared event must not be assigned to whichever
execution happens to be current. An execution-private callback must not become
shared merely because its execution was retired.

The declaration of an event as shared is part of the checked contract. An
untrusted field such as =:shared? true= cannot select weaker admission rules.
Shared admission permits only the declared shared transition; it does not
restore a retired execution's private ownership or authorize installation of
domain content carried by an advisory.

The browser-realization part defines the concrete normalization, transition,
and effect contracts implementing this boundary. All such paths obey this
owner-relative rule; there is no alternate callback path that may mutate
semantic state without admission.



** Trust boundaries and adversarial browser actors
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-trust-boundary
:END:

A browser is an untrusted participant.

This statement is stronger than saying browser input should be validated.

The user controls the machine and may modify:

#+begin_example
gesso-live.js
projected choreography data
local machine state
DOM
hidden fields
command envelopes
role names
principal ids
observed basis values
provenance labels
provisional/authoritative markers
available buttons
bound capabilities
diagnostic/proof sidecars
runtime correlation fields
#+end_example

Therefore no browser-originated value may confer trusted authority merely
because it has the expected representation.

*** What the browser may propose

A browser may propose values such as:

- a semantic operation;
- operation arguments;
- a semantic command identity;
- an observed-basis claim used as protocol context;
- correlation information whose validity is checked independently;
- provisional local state affecting only its own representation;
- a projected-plan identity whose compatibility is checked by trusted code;
- transport data required to correlate a request with a server-side operation.

Proposal is not authority.

*** What the browser may not establish by assertion

The browser may not establish by assertion:

- authenticated principal identity;
- entitlement to a choreography role;
- model authority;
- authorization to operate on a protected target;
- authoritative provenance;
- an authoritative fact;
- a privileged server-machine state;
- permission to read protected data;
- permission to subscribe to protected Live state;
- the truth of a proof certificate;
- that a stale execution is current;
- that a provisional projection is authoritative;
- that an observed-basis claim proves prior observation.

Trusted server code must derive or check those propositions through trusted
boundaries.

*** Trusted authoritative execution

For each protected authoritative operation, the supported trusted path must
establish the following obligations. Their exact physical ordering may depend
on the operation, but no protected action may occur before its required premises
have been established:

1. establish authenticated principal identity from trusted server context;
2. resolve the requested semantic operation through the trusted current
   application/operation registry;
3. establish the permitted server-side trajectory rather than accepting a
   client-supplied privileged machine state;
4. re-evaluate current authorization and relevant model policy;
5. read the authoritative facts required by the public model operation;
6. invoke the public model operation that owns the authoritative transition;
7. produce only the settlement/progression outcomes admitted by the declared
   operation contract.

An authorization decision or relevant precondition established during
preparation must be protected or re-established at the authoritative transition
boundary wherever an intervening change could invalidate the operation. The
atomic-operation contract defines those guards; browser freshness does not
supply them.

The server must never treat a client-supplied machine continuation such as:

#+begin_src clojure
{:next-state :request-authority/commit-admin-operation}
#+end_src

as evidence that the client is entitled to reach that state.

Instead the browser requests a semantic operation, conceptually:

#+begin_src clojure
{:command-id C42
 :operation :request/claim
 :arguments {:request-id R}
 :observed-basis X17}
#+end_src

and trusted server semantics decide what operation and continuation are
permitted.

*** Security boundary

A malicious browser can always lie to itself.

It may render false information, suppress buttons, manufacture buttons, change
labels, or locally pretend an operation succeeded.

Preventing a user from corrupting their own local representation is not the
security target.

The security target is:

#+begin_quote
Compromising or replacing a browser projection may corrupt that browser's local
representation and protocol participation, but must not increase the authority
of its authenticated principal, disclose data unavailable to that principal, or
produce an authoritative model transition unavailable to that principal.
#+end_quote

This is a verification target, not merely an application coding convention.

*** Read and subscription authorization

Hiding content in a view is not access control.

Not rendering a button is not access control.

Not advertising a Live interest is not access control.

Every protected authoritative observation and subscription must be authorized
from trusted server context.

An opaque or signed token may protect integrity, correlation, or accidental
mutation.

Possession of such a token is not automatically authorization unless an
explicit capability design says that possession has that semantic meaning and
its applicability and validity conditions are satisfied.

The protected-observation and subscription contracts must state when
authorization is checked and what later permission changes imply for subsequent
disclosure. Initial subscription admission must not silently become an
indefinite permission grant. The protocol may use revalidation, bounded grants,
revocation, or another explicit policy consistent with the claimed protection.
No such policy can make an adversarial browser forget data already disclosed.

*** Proof artifacts are untrusted in the browser

A proof sidecar or diagnostic certificate shipped to a browser cannot become a
security capability.

An adversarial browser may fabricate, modify, remove, or replace it.

Authoritative security therefore depends on trusted server-side state and
checked protocol boundaries, not the browser's possession of proof metadata.

This follows the general rule:

#+begin_example
evidence about why code was accepted
!=
runtime authority to perform operation
#+end_example

*** Trusted host code and accidental-misuse prevention

The adversarial-browser boundary must not be confused with a claim that Gesso
sandboxes arbitrary Clojure code executing inside a trusted application
process.

Code that deliberately obtains a lower-level transaction capability, database
handle, model implementation namespace, or another authority-bearing resource
may be able to bypass the supported Gesso/model path.  If such code can mutate
the same authoritative state directly, it belongs to the trusted computing
base for every guarantee that depends on those model invariants unless a
stronger isolation boundary prevents the bypass.

Within that trusted host-language boundary, Gesso is primarily concerned with
preventing *accidental semantic misuse* by cooperating application code.  The
normal path should make incorrect substitutions, missing derivations, stale
artifacts, undeclared authority paths, and accidental bypasses difficult to
express and easy to diagnose.

The intended progression is:

#+begin_example
ordinary supported application code
    -> narrow semantic/model interfaces
    -> checked construction and runtime premises
    -> authoritative model operation

accidental mismatch
    -> early specific rejection

deliberate lower-level bypass by trusted host code
    -> explicit exit from the corresponding Gesso assurance claim
#+end_example

Architectural restrictions may reduce that trusted surface further.  For
example, application assembly, dependency checks, narrow capabilities, or
namespace discipline may make ordinary view/handler code unable to reach a
transaction primitive through the supported construction path.  Later
whole-application assurance specifies what such enforcement establishes.

These mechanisms are valuable even though a sufficiently determined trusted
application author can rewrite the program or deliberately acquire a lower-level
handle.  The target is not to make trusted code physically incapable of
cheating.  The target is to make the coherent path obvious, accidental misuse
hard, and deliberate escape from the claimed boundary conspicuous.

** Knowledge
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-knowledge-provenance
:END:

Knowledge is tracked with respect to actors or projected roles.

A fact or value may become known through several distinct provenance paths.

At minimum the semantic model recognizes:

- =:input=;
- =:communicated=;
- =:authoritative=;
- =:derived=;
- =:asserted=.

These labels describe justification, not merely transport. Their appearance in
an incoming map does not establish the corresponding provenance. A knowledge
judgment includes the subject and scope of the fact and the premises on which
the permitted use depends; a provenance label alone is not the whole judgment.

The important verifier question is not:

#+begin_quote
Which packet delivered this map key?
#+end_quote

It is:

#+begin_quote
Before this actor performs behavior that depends on fact F, what justifies that
the actor knows F?
#+end_quote

*** Input knowledge

Input knowledge is justified knowledge supplied at operation entry.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
F in declared inputs(A)
-----------------------
A knows F
provenance: input
#+end_example

Input knowledge is not equivalent to arbitrary function arguments.

The operation contract must say which incoming facts are accepted as justified
entry knowledge and under what assumptions.

For example, a trusted server may supply an authenticated principal as input
knowledge to a server projection.

A browser cannot create the same trusted input merely by serializing an
identical principal id.

*** Communicated knowledge

Communication may establish knowledge constructively.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
A knows X
A sends declared X to B
B receives declared X
-----------------------
B knows X
provenance: communicated
#+end_example

An admitted choreography must justify the sender's knowledge of the value it
transmits. At an adversarial transport boundary, a message's receipt does not
prove that the sender satisfied that semantic premise; trusted validation or
the explicitly stated assumption must supply the justification being claimed.

The receiver gains only the values declared by the message contract.

Arbitrary extra map keys must not silently become choreography context.

Communication establishes the fact that the declared value was communicated in
that execution; it does not automatically establish that the value is
authoritative domain truth.

*** Authoritative knowledge

An actor may acquire knowledge by observing a representation justified by
authority.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
authority establishes authoritative F
B observes an authoritative projection containing F
at a basis sufficient to justify that projection
--------------------------------------------------
B knows authoritative F at that observation frontier
provenance: authoritative
#+end_example

The physical representation may be:

- structured data;
- server-rendered HTML;
- another checked projection;
- an application-specific authoritative representation.

The representation format does not weaken the provenance if the trusted path
from authority to observation is valid.

*** Derived knowledge

A role may derive a fact from other facts it already knows through a declared
deterministic derivation.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
B knows X
B knows Y
D requires X and Y
D deterministically produces F under its declared semantic contract
--------------------------------
B knows F
provenance: derived
#+end_example

A derivation must declare enough information for the verifier/checker to know
which inputs justify the result.

The system must not assume arbitrary separately executed Clojure functions
produce equal results across actors unless that relationship is justified by
the declared semantics. Any clock, randomness, external read, locale, or other
environmental input affecting the result must be supplied as a declared input
or remain an explicit assumption; a function name does not establish determinism.

Derivation preserves the premises on which the result depends. Deriving a value
from a client assertion does not silently make the result authoritative. A
derived result can support a stronger claim only when the declared derivation
rule and its premises justify that claim.

*** Asserted knowledge

An explicit assertion supplies knowledge the verifier does not derive.

This is a legitimate escape hatch for facts whose truth Gesso cannot establish.

The assertion must remain visible as a trusted premise.

It must not be silently promoted to:

- =:derived=;
- =:communicated=;
- =:authoritative=.

The application should be able to explain which behavior depends on the
assertion.

*** Knowledge use

Knowledge determines whether a role may, depending on the operation:

- transmit a value;
- consume a value;
- use a value as an FX input;
- correlate a message;
- choose a branch;
- rely on a discriminator when its projected behavior diverges;
- derive another value;
- construct provisional state;
- invoke an operation whose entry contract requires the fact;
- claim agreement with another role;
- establish a checked rule precondition.

A role must not consume facts it cannot justifiably know.

*** Knowledge is semantic, not incidental context

The presence of a key in a runtime map is not evidence of knowledge.

For example:

#+begin_src clojure
{:user/id U
 :request/status :open}
#+end_src

does not answer:

- who knows those values;
- why they know them;
- whether they are authoritative;
- at what basis they were observed;
- whether they may be transmitted;
- whether they may justify a branch;
- whether they are merely local provisional data.

The semantic machinery must not infer provenance from incidental map
membership.

*** Knowledge and privacy

Knowledge is scoped.

Correctness does not require every actor to learn every fact.

Privacy, authorization, role relevance, and protocol structure may deliberately
prevent knowledge propagation.

A proof that "eventually everybody knows everything" would be wrong for many
applications.

The useful property is:

#+begin_quote
Every actor knows enough, with sufficient justification, for the behavior it is
permitted to perform.
#+end_quote

*** Knowledge derivations as inspectable evidence

Where knowledge is machine checked, the checker should retain enough derivation
information to explain why a fact is known.

A boolean such as:

#+begin_src clojure
{:knows? true}
#+end_src

is insufficient as the long-term proof product.

The derivation should be able to expose leaves such as:

- input;
- communication;
- authoritative observation;
- deterministic derivation;
- explicit assertion.

These derivations belong to verifier/checker artifacts.

They do not need to be shipped as production browser state.

** Authoritative observation as a knowledge path
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-authoritative-observation
:END:

Authoritative observation is a first-class semantic provenance path even when
it is not represented as a first-class portable machine opcode.

This distinction is important for ordinary web applications.

A HumanHelp browser may learn facts such as:

#+begin_example
Request R is approved
Request R is claimed
Membership U is inactive
Organization O is closed
#+end_example

by rereading an authoritative representation after Live indicates that relevant
authority may have changed.

That knowledge is not weaker merely because it arrived as rendered HTML rather
than as a dedicated choreography participant message.

The authority remains in the model/database.

The HTML is one representation from which the actor learns the fact.

*** Advisory invalidation versus authoritative observation

The normal causal relationship, when the application policy permits the
acquisition, is:

#+begin_example
authoritative mutation
        |
        v
model / XTDB
        |
        v
Live invalidation
        |
        v
application-permitted acquisition trigger
        |
        v
authorized authoritative reread
        |
        v
actor acquires newer authoritative knowledge
#+end_example

A Live invalidation means approximately:

#+begin_quote
Relevant authority may have changed; a sufficiently current authoritative
observation may be required for later behavior or offered for explicit adoption.
#+end_quote

It does not mean:

#+begin_quote
This particular domain fact is now true.
#+end_quote

Therefore invalidation is advisory knowledge about possible change, not
authoritative knowledge of the changed fact.

Receipt of the advisory does not by itself choose when the application adopts
new content. Acquisition may follow an explicit user action, a declared
automatic policy, or another admitted trigger. Any convergence claim that relies
on such a trigger must name it among its liveness premises.

*** Observation scope and selective incorporation

An authoritative observation establishes only the facts and coverage justified
by its acquisition contract. Its semantic context includes the authority,
subject or projection scope, relevant basis/progression, and applicable temporal
or query meaning. These need not all be repeated as fields when they are
unambiguously supplied by the admitted contract.

A later observation of one Request does not establish acquisition of all board
membership at the same database progression. An actor may hold individually
justified observations from different acquisitions without possessing a single
consistent snapshot of their union.

Consequently, installation of new authoritative content must preserve each
observation's justified scope. Claims spanning multiple observations require
the compatibility or consistent-acquisition premise appropriate to that claim.
The mere existence of separate authoritative observations is insufficient.

The authoritative-operations and progression part owns the detailed
[[#req-progression-scope][scope and progression contract]] and
[[#req-advisory-discharge][advisory discharge rules]]. Browser installation follows
[[#req-runtime-scoped-authoritative-installation][the scoped installation contract]].
HumanHelp's selective-adoption policy is a use of those generic contracts.


*** Observed basis is protocol context, not proof of prior observation
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-observation-evidence
:END:

A naked browser-supplied =:observed-basis= value is a client claim about the
context from which the browser believes it is acting.  It is not, by itself,
proof that the browser actually received or incorporated an authoritative
observation at that basis.

For ordinary Gesso commands this is intentional.  The basis is useful protocol
context for consistency, reconciliation, provenance, stale-command handling,
and authoritative reacquisition even when the browser is adversarial.

For example, a browser may say:

#+begin_src clojure
{:request-id R
 :observed-basis B}
#+end_src

and the server may safely use =B= to understand the command's claimed starting
context without treating =B= as permission:

#+begin_example
client says it acted from B
        |
        v
server compares B with current authority
        |
        +-- compatible/current enough -> continue under normal model guards
        |
        +-- stale/incompatible -> reconcile, reject, or reacquire
#+end_example

Current authenticated principal binding, model authorization, authoritative
state, and transaction guards remain decisive.  A client may lie about =B=
without thereby gaining authority.

The trust requirement changes if an application gives *prior observation
itself* security significance.  Examples include:

- an operation is allowed only after the principal was previously issued a
  particular authoritative representation;
- a continuation is valid only when it follows a specific server-issued
  observation;
- an acknowledgement must refer to an exact previously delivered semantic
  version;
- an application distinguishes "client claims to have seen X" from
  "the server can verify that it issued X to this principal/session."

In that case a forgeable client field is insufficient.  The application needs
an explicit server-verifiable mechanism such as:

- a server-issued capability;
- a MAC-authenticated observation receipt;
- trusted server-side session state;
- a signed server attestation;
- a nonce or continuation identifier bound to a prior response;
- another mechanism whose trust semantics are stated explicitly.

The framework must never silently promote:

#+begin_example
ClientClaim<ObservedBasis>
#+end_example

into:

#+begin_example
VerifiedObservationEvidence
#+end_example

merely because the value has the expected shape or happens to equal a real
basis.

A Message Authentication Code (MAC), for example HMAC-SHA-256, is one possible
way to let an adversarial browser carry integrity-protected server evidence,
under the named cryptographic, key-management, encoding, and verification
assumptions. This example specifies the trust relationship rather than a
complete cryptographic implementation.  The server authenticates a canonical payload
using a secret key known only to trusted infrastructure:

#+begin_example
receipt-payload
    =
{:principal-id P
 :authority [:request R]
 :projection :request/public-row
 :basis B
 :purpose :prior-observation
 :issued-at I
 :expires-at E}

mac
    =
HMAC(server-secret, canonical-encoding(receipt-payload))
#+end_example

The browser may read, store, copy, and return the payload and MAC. Under the
declared integrity assumptions it cannot feasibly change =P=, =R=, =B=,
=purpose=, or another authenticated field and produce accepted replacement
evidence. The MAC does not encrypt the payload; receipt contents must therefore
be suitable for disclosure to the recipient.

On receipt, the server recomputes and verifies the MAC and then checks the
context in which the receipt is being used:

#+begin_example
untrusted client-carried bytes
        |
verify MAC
        |
verify principal / authority / purpose / expiry
        |
        v
server-authenticated prior-observation evidence
#+end_example

The MAC authenticates the statement represented by the payload; it does not
make an unjustified statement true.  If the server authenticates a bad or
overstated proposition, the MAC faithfully preserves that mistake.

In particular, a receipt normally proves something like:

#+begin_quote
Trusted server infrastructure issued an attestation for principal/session P
about the declared semantic observation of authority A at basis B, under the
receipt's stated issuance contract.
#+end_quote

The issuance contract must identify the event actually attested: for example,
construction of an authorized response or release of a response to a transport.
Issuance is not proof of successful delivery, browser installation, retention,
or human acknowledgement. Returning the receipt proves possession of the
authenticated bytes, not those stronger events by itself.

It does not by itself prove:

#+begin_quote
The browser installed the observation, or the human read, understood, or
consciously incorporated it.
#+end_quote

If human acknowledgement is semantically relevant, that is a stronger protocol
and needs its own explicit proposition and evidence.

Observation evidence must also remain distinct from an operation capability.
A valid authenticated receipt concerning an old authoritative observation may
remain historically valid even after current authority changes:

#+begin_example
valid observation receipt
!=
current authorization
#+end_example

For example, another helper may have claimed Request R after the receipt was
issued.  The receipt can still prove that an earlier observation was issued,
while current authoritative reread and model guards correctly reject a new
claim.

Gesso should therefore distinguish at least these concepts:

#+begin_example
ObservedBasisClaim
    client supplied
    forgeable
    useful for consistency/reconciliation

AuthenticatedObservationReceipt
    server authenticated
    evidence about a prior server-issued observation

CurrentAuthoritativeObservation
    trusted acquisition satisfying its declared scope and consistency contract
    current relative to that acquisition, not a promise of perpetual freshness

OperationCapability
    explicitly scoped permission under declared conditions

CurrentAuthorization
    current trusted decision that a concrete principal may perform the
    operation against current authoritative state
#+end_example

None should implicitly coerce into another.  In particular, historical
observation evidence and possession of an operation capability do not silently
become current authorization unless the declared capability semantics explicitly
make possession sufficient and all of that capability's current validity
conditions are satisfied.

Gesso may emit compact authenticated observation receipts alongside
authoritative acquisitions when a supported protocol benefits from evidence of
prior issuance. Routine issuance is an implementation option, not a baseline
requirement justified solely by the cost of computing a MAC. Its key lifecycle,
payload disclosure, expiry, and replay contract must be explicit.  The authenticated object
should be the *semantic observation* or a compact digest/identity of it, not
arbitrary HTTP response bytes.

For example:

#+begin_src clojure
{:receipt/version 1
 :principal-id P
 :authority [:request R]
 :projection :request/card
 :basis B
 :semantic-digest D
 :issued-at I
 :expires-at E}
#+end_src

could be MACed as one canonical receipt.

Even if such receipts become common, ordinary =:observed-basis= should remain a
distinct weaker protocol concept.  Requiring cryptographic evidence whenever a
client reports its starting basis would unnecessarily turn useful consistency
context into a security credential.

Replay semantics also remain explicit.  A MAC makes a token difficult to forge
or alter; it does not make the token single-use.  A reusable observation
receipt may legitimately be replayable because it attests to a historical
fact.  A one-shot capability must enforce its declared consumption or replay policy.
Expiry limits validity duration but does not by itself prevent reuse within
that duration. A nonce distinguishes issuances but does not by itself record
consumption. A consumed-token record or another trusted mechanism may enforce
single use; command identity and idempotency may instead enforce a permitted
duplicate-use outcome. Those are different contracts and must not be presented
as interchangeable guarantees.

The governing rule is:

#+begin_quote
When prior observation becomes a premise of authorization or another
security-sensitive conclusion, the mechanism establishing that premise must
cross an explicit server-verifiable trust boundary.  Client assertion remains
client assertion until that boundary is crossed.
#+end_quote

*** Observation need not imply a long-lived waiting opcode

Many authoritative observations form entry knowledge for a later short
choreography.

For example:

#+begin_example
browser renders authoritative Request at X17
    |
execution ends

...later...

Live advisory arrives
    |
application policy or explicit user action permits acquisition
    |
browser performs authorized authoritative reread at X24
    |
new short choreography begins with knowledge from X24
#+end_example

The portable choreography language therefore need not acquire a universal
=:observe= or durable wait operation merely to model ordinary web rereads.

If an application genuinely needs one semantic execution to suspend on an
authoritative condition and later resume, that facility should be designed
deliberately with explicit persistence, compatibility, ownership, and recovery
semantics.

It must not appear accidentally as a consequence of normal Live behavior.

*** Observation and representation independence

Authoritative observation is semantic.

Its correctness must not depend on one specific presentation format.

A browser may acquire justified knowledge from an authoritative HTML projection
when the server-rendered representation and its acquisition path establish the
required facts.

Another application may use structured data.

The important proof obligations concern:

- authority;
- authorization;
- basis/progression;
- projection semantics;
- correspondence between representation and declared fact.

They do not concern whether the bytes happened to be HTML or EDN unless the
representation mechanism itself introduces a relevant assumption.

** Agreement
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-semantic-agreement
:END:

Agreement is stronger than two actors having values with the same shape.

Agreement means the protocol can justify that the actors share the relevant
semantic value or fact under the declared relation, scope, and frontier.

This is a judgment about those actors' relevant observations or values at the
specified points. It does not automatically mean that either actor knows the
other actor received the value, that both know that both know it, or that they
continue to share the same current value after later changes. Protocols needing
acknowledgement or stronger knowledge about receipt must declare that additional
obligation.

If A communicates X to B:

#+begin_example
A knows X
A communicates declared X to B
B receives declared X
--------------------------------
A's sent observation and B's received observation agree on declared X
provenance: communicated, subject to the message contract's premises
#+end_example

Agreement can arise through multiple provenance paths.

At minimum:

- =:communicated=;
- =:authoritative=;
- =:derived=;
- =:asserted=.

*** Communicated agreement

Communication can establish agreement on the communicated value.

It does not imply agreement on every fact either actor knows.

Message contracts therefore matter.

Closed payloads make the agreement boundary explicit.

*** Authoritative agreement

Two actors may independently observe the same authoritative fact and obtain
justified agreement without receiving one identical network packet.

For example:

#+begin_example
authority establishes F

actor A
  reads F through web node 1
  at compatible authoritative frontier

actor B
  reads F through web node 3
  at compatible authoritative frontier

=> A and B may have authoritative agreement on F
#+end_example

The hosts differ.

The packets differ.

The semantic authority and relevant fact agree. The required agreement is on
the scoped proposition being used, not necessarily on the exact read timestamp
or complete database snapshot. Equal progression tokens alone do not establish
agreement when the subjects or projection meanings differ.

*** Derived agreement

Agreement may propagate through a declared deterministic derivation when the
inputs and derivation justify doing so.

The system must not infer derived agreement merely because two actors happen to
run source code with the same function name.

The derivation relation must be part of the checked semantic contract.

*** Asserted agreement

Application code may explicitly assert agreement that Gesso cannot derive.

Such assertions are trusted leaves.

They must remain visible in diagnostics and proof reports.

They should not be used casually to suppress useful obligations.

*** Shape equality is not agreement

Two values can validate against the same Malli schema without being agreed
semantic facts.

For example:

#+begin_src clojure
{:request/status :open}
#+end_src

in two browser contexts does not establish:

- same Request identity;
- same authoritative basis;
- same provenance;
- same valid time;
- same semantic observation;
- same authority.

Malli answers shape questions.

Agreement answers semantic relationship questions.

*** Agreement is scoped

Actors need not agree on all application state.

Agreement may be scoped to:

- a Request;
- a fact;
- a command;
- an operation input;
- a branch discriminator;
- an authoritative frontier;
- another explicitly modeled semantic object.

Privacy and irrelevance are legitimate reasons not to establish agreement.

** Agreement frontiers
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-agreement-frontiers
:END:

For evolving values, actors may agree only through a common frontier for the
relevant facts and relation. A frontier is not necessarily one globally ordered
position for all actor knowledge.

For a single modeled chain whose prefix observations are retained and justify
the claimed agreement, for example:

#+begin_example
F1 -> F2 -> F3 -> F4

Actor A knows through F4.
Actor B knows through F3.

A and B agree through F3.
#+end_example

The application universe may change continually.

Complete instantaneous agreement among all relevant actors may never occur.

That is not itself a correctness failure.

Correctness instead requires:

- established historical facts remain immutable under the model's semantics;
- relevant divergence is explicit rather than silently erased;
- actors possess enough justified knowledge for the behavior they perform;
- where convergence is required, the protocol provides a valid path toward a
  newer common authoritative frontier;
- under quiescence and the choreography's stated liveness assumptions, required
  agreement debt can fall to zero.

*** Frontier is relation, not necessarily arithmetic

A frontier need not be represented by an integer.

The browser must not invent arithmetic ordering over opaque progression values
unless the authoritative system explicitly provides a supported ordering
operation.

The relevant semantic questions are relational:

- is this observation at least as strong as the requirement that triggered it?
- are two authoritative observations compatible for the fact whose agreement
  is being claimed?
- has this actor incorporated the authoritative change it was required to
  observe?
- may this local projection continue under the current compatibility contract?

The concrete XTDB and scope-coverage mechanisms are specified in the
authoritative-operations and progression part. An ordering relation must not be
assumed to provide a join, a greatest common frontier, or complete observation
coverage unless the supported contract actually establishes those properties.

*** Different actors may have different valid frontiers

For example:

#+begin_example
Authority:
    F1 -> F2 -> F3 -> F4 -> F5

Actor A:
    knows through F5

Actor B:
    knows through F3

Actor C:
    knows only a privacy-scoped subset through F4
#+end_example

There may be no meaningful statement that all three actors share one universal
application frontier.

Agreement and knowledge are scoped to the relevant facts and authorities.

*** Advisory knowledge and adopted knowledge

An actor may learn that authority advanced without immediately adopting the new
authoritative projection.

This distinction is important for application behaviors such as a multi-user
board:

#+begin_example
other client learns:
    "new relevant Request authority is available"

but does not yet replace:
    currently adopted board projection

explicit refresh:
    acquires and adopts newer authoritative projection
#+end_example

The advisory requirements and adopted authoritative observations are therefore
distinct pieces of actor state. The word frontier is shorthand for the scoped
knowledge relation; it does not require either state to be represented by one
maximum scalar token.

A correct application may intentionally preserve that distinction.

*** Convergence does not require replay of every intermediate change

A browser offline from X17 through X900 need not replay every invalidation
between those points.

If the application semantics permit it, the actor may converge by acquiring a
current authoritative observation at X900 or later.

The correctness obligation is not "deliver every historical notification."

It is:

#+begin_quote
Provide a valid path by which the actor can acquire sufficiently current
authoritative knowledge for the behavior it needs to perform.
#+end_quote

*** Agreement debt

Where the choreography requires actors to converge, the difference between
their relevant justified knowledge may be understood as agreement debt.

The design does not require one universal numeric debt metric.

The useful property is qualitative:

- divergence is represented;
- required convergence paths exist;
- stale or provisional actors do not pretend agreement already exists;
- under stated liveness assumptions and quiescence, the required divergence can
  be eliminated.

The existence of a recovery path is distinct from its eventual execution.
Where adoption requires a user to activate Refresh, required eventual adoption
is conditional on that action or another permitted acquisition trigger. Where
the product permits indefinite deferral, remaining advisory information is not
by itself a violation of an unconditional convergence guarantee; such a
guarantee was never established for that deferred adoption.

*** Privacy constrains convergence

Agreement is always scoped by authorization and relevance.

The system must not satisfy a convergence property by leaking protected facts to
actors that are not entitled to observe them.

A correct convergence path may therefore involve:

- one actor learning the full authoritative fact;
- another actor learning only an authorized projection;
- both actors agreeing on a smaller shared semantic proposition.

For example:

#+begin_example
authority knows:
    full Request details + private requester metadata

helper A may know:
    Request is available for claim

observer B may know:
    Request count increased

A and B need not agree on private requester metadata.
#+end_example

*** Foundational invariant

The foundational agreement invariant is:

#+begin_quote
A Gesso execution never treats mere co-presence, shape equality, physical
delivery, or coincidental local computation as semantic agreement.  Agreement
must be justified by a declared communication, compatible authoritative
observation, checked derivation, or explicit trusted assertion, and any claim
over evolving facts is relative to a meaningful common frontier.
#+end_quote
