* Part VI — Browser realization and recovery
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: part-06-browser-realization-and-recovery
:END:

This part defines the pure browser adapter, its HTMX and physical-effect
boundaries, fragment coordination, stale-plan recovery, managed effects,
asynchronous admission, and authoritative absence. It applies the ownership
rules of Part II, the scoped acquisition and command contracts of Part IV, and
the executable/presentation boundaries of Part V. Target theorems identify
required assurance; they do not claim that implementation or proof is complete.

** Browser adapter refinement
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-browser-adapter-refinement
:END:

*** Executable semantic core and browser integration boundary

The browser adapter must be structured so the Gesso semantic part is small and
pure. This does not require Gesso to replace, wrap, or reimplement HTMX's normal
request and DOM lifecycle.

Normalized Gesso-relevant events enter a deterministic transition function:

#+begin_example
AdapterStep : ExecutablePlan x AdapterState x NormalizedEvent
           -> AdapterState x AbstractEffects
#+end_example

The plan supplies immutable executable rules; state and admitted events supply
concrete runtime values, following [[#req-executable-plan-sidecar-boundary][Part V]].
An implementation may bind the plan before invoking the step function, but may
not replace it with a diagnostic or test-only semantic object.

This adapter is an executable semantic core for browser coordination. The
portable execution machine owns its projected trajectory; the two cores compose
through their declared state/effect boundary. A proof kernel checks derivations
about the semantics, and a dedicated semantic checker establishes its particular
judgment. Neither supplies runtime facts or replaces concrete event admission.

Representative normalized events include:

#+begin_src clojure
{:event :htmx/before-request
 :execution-id E
 :transport-request-id H}

{:event :htmx/before-swap
 :execution-id E
 :transport-request-id H
 :target-id T}

{:event :htmx/after-swap
 :execution-id E
 :transport-request-id H
 :target-id T}

{:event :http/failed
 :execution-id E
 :transport-request-id H
 :error X}

{:event :live/invalidated
 :owner [:fragment F]
 :source-instance S
 :requirement R}

{:event :timer/fired
 :execution-id E
 :timer-id T
 :resource-instance I}

{:event :continuity/completed
 :owner O
 :resource-instance I
 :slot S}
#+end_src

These are illustrative event shapes, not complete wire schemas. In particular,
the shared advisory resolves its declared source and scoped requirement without
requiring a command execution. H identifies one transport attempt, not a
HumanHelp domain Request; a retry has a distinct transport-request identity even
when it retains the command identity. In these examples H also identifies the
concrete request resource, so a redundant resource-instance field is unnecessary.
For the timer, T names the logical timer and I identifies its concrete instance.
The continuity owner may be an execution or an admitted fragment/effect scope. Correlation fields propose information for
admission; their mere presence does not establish ownership.

Representative abstract effects/dispositions include:

#+begin_src clojure
[:machine/resume ...]
[:htmx/allow-request ...]
[:htmx/cancel-request ...]
[:htmx/allow-swap ...]
[:htmx/cancel-swap ...]
[:timer/start ...]
[:timer/cancel ...]
[:dom/install-provisional ...]
[:execution/retire ...]
#+end_src

For HTMX-owned operations, Gesso should normally decide semantic disposition--for
example allow, cancel, ignore as stale, correlate, or observe completion--while
HTMX continues to perform the request, response parsing, OOB handling, DOM swap,
and settle work it already owns.

HTMX lifecycle listeners, EventSource/SSE observations, timers, MutationObserver
callbacks, and Gesso-owned DOM hooks should submit Gesso-relevant observations to
the executable semantic core rather than independently deciding choreography semantics,
mutating execution ownership, or resuming machines through alternative code
paths.

Gesso-owned physical effects that are not HTMX's responsibility, such as local
provisional rendering, timers, continuity resources, and related cleanup, are
interpreted by a narrow imperative effect shell. That shell performs physical
browser operations but does not invent semantic state transitions.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
                ordinary HTMX lifecycle
                         |
              documented lifecycle event
                         |
                         v
                  checked AdapterStep
                         |
                 allow/cancel/observe
                         |
                         v
                HTMX continues normally
                         |
              after-swap / after-settle
                         |
                         v
                  checked AdapterStep

Gesso-owned browser event ---> checked AdapterStep ---> narrow browser FFI effect
#+end_example

Here =checked= identifies the transition object to which the stated assurance
applies. It is not a claim that formal proof is already complete. The actual
evidence class and remaining correspondence assumptions must be reported.

This architecture is a correctness requirement, not merely a code-style
preference. It deliberately preserves HTMX as a useful declarative runtime
instead of turning it into a transport primitive beneath a Gesso reimplementation.

*** HTMX integration contract

HTMX is a trusted external runtime, not a Gesso effect implementation.

Gesso should use HTMX through its ordinary declarative API and documented
lifecycle events. HTMX remains responsible for the behavior it already owns,
including ordinary request execution, response processing, out-of-band response
handling, swaps, and settlement.

Gesso may observe or intervene at documented lifecycle boundaries when a
Gesso-owned semantic invariant requires it. Typical intervention is narrow:
allow the lifecycle to continue, cancel/suppress stale work, attach or recover
opaque correlation, capture continuity information, or observe completion.

A custom HTMX extension is NOT required by this architecture. Gesso should not
introduce one merely to make HTMX look like an abstract effect executor. If a
future concrete requirement cannot be implemented correctly through HTMX's
ordinary declarative API and documented lifecycle hooks, an extension may be
considered then as an explicit engineering choice with its own proof/test
boundary.

The exact HTMX behavior on which Gesso relies forms a supported-contract subset.
The implementation should pin and test the HTMX and htmx-ext-sse versions it
supports and document any response headers, swap modes, OOB behavior, history
features, or other options that can bypass or alter a lifecycle gate required by
Gesso correctness. Unsupported combinations may be rejected rather than silently
weakening the semantic guarantees.

For each supported response/swap mode, the integration must identify which
documented gate precedes every relevant physical mutation and which event
establishes completion or failure. Main-target approval must not implicitly
authorize unrelated out-of-band targets, history restoration, or other effects
whose ownership and scope have not been admitted. Response parsing alone is
not authoritative installation.

If a required action cannot be suppressed before it affects protected semantic
presentation under the selected lifecycle contract, that configuration cannot
claim the corresponding invariant. The integration must reject it or provide a
separately specified realization with the required correspondence evidence.
These requirements do not assert particular hook behavior for an unexamined
HTMX version.

The proof does not claim HTMX itself is correct. It claims that, assuming the
supported HTMX lifecycle contract, Gesso's semantic decisions at those boundaries
preserve the stated adapter invariants.

*** Browser FFI contract

The Gesso-owned browser FFI/effect shell is an explicit proof assumption and must
remain deliberately weaker than the semantic adapter core. Its job is physical
execution of Gesso-owned browser effects and faithful translation of primitive
browser observations, not protocol reasoning. HTMX's native lifecycle is governed
by the separate HTMX integration contract above rather than being reimplemented
inside this shell.

The FFI shell may:

- observe primitive browser events and normalize their raw payloads;
- carry opaque owner/resource correlation identity installed when semantic
  logic created or admitted the physical resource;
- report physical success, failure, cancellation, or disappearance as declared
  normalized events;
- perform concrete Gesso-owned DOM, EventSource, timer, request, and listener
  operations requested by admitted abstract effects, without independently
  duplicating an HTMX-owned request or swap;
- record diagnostics about physical failures.

The FFI shell must not:

- infer semantic authority or authorization;
- infer domain facts merely from arbitrary DOM text or client-provided labels;
- choose an execution or shared owner by independent semantic reasoning;
- invent an admitted execution/resource identity or upgrade provenance,
  authority, basis, or ownership;
- decide whether rollback, reconciliation, supersession, or retirement is valid;
- transfer semantic ownership between executions;
- mutate semantic adapter state through a side channel;
- resume a projected machine except through an abstract effect emitted by the
  adapter transition core.

Correlation should be established when semantic logic creates or admits a
physical resource and then carried opaquely by integration code. Browser code
must not reconstruct semantic ownership later from mutable DOM position, CSS
selectors, current target contents, or another physical coincidence.

A physical effect must not rely on an admission decision that has already lost
its applicability. The execution contract must either perform the action within
the admitted serialized step or validate its still-current permit/ownership at
the last required boundary before mutation. A delayed effect queued while E1
was current cannot install obsolete DOM after E1 retires merely because its
later completion callback would be rejected.

This is a boundary check, not permission for the shell to independently decide
ownership. The adapter owns the permit and its validity rule. Physical handles
must remain associated with the resource instance created for them so cleanup
cannot rediscover and remove a newer resource by selector alone.

The smaller this shell remains, the smaller the unproved correspondence boundary
between the formal adapter and the real browser. Chromium integration tests are
responsible for aggressively challenging both these assumptions and the supported
HTMX lifecycle contract.

*** Property/model-based transition testing

Property- and model-based testing should be used early while the transition
semantics are still being designed. This is complementary to proof, not a
replacement for it.

For example, a generator may produce sequences such as:

#+begin_example
start E1
request begins
invalidation arrives
retire E1
start E2
E1 response arrives
E1 timer fires
E2 response arrives
duplicate E1 response arrives
continuity callback arrives
#+end_example

and check the adapter invariants after every transition.

Useful generated properties include execution isolation, retired-resource
rejection, declared fragment-coordination bounds, semantic ownership uniqueness,
prevention of obsolete restoration after authoritative installation, and terminal retirement.

A generated counterexample can expose a bad invariant or transition rule before
time is spent proving it. Passing generated tests does not justify the word
=proved=; the same claimed safety property still requires the mechanical proof
defined by this document.

*** Adapter refinement theorem

The target claim is approximately:

#+begin_quote
Assuming the declared browser primitives and supported HTMX lifecycle contract
satisfy their named assumptions, every semantic transition made by the Gesso
browser adapter corresponds to a permitted transition of the formal
browser/Choreo semantics, and the adapter preserves its stated safety
invariants.
#+end_quote

The production transition core should be generated from or directly implement
the checked transition representation. A stronger formal claim additionally
requires the production/formal correspondence evidence for that representation
and checker. A separately handwritten semantic callback layer would reopen the
gap; shared terminology or similar source code is not correspondence evidence.

The abstract event model must include stale, duplicate, malformed, missing, and
reordered observations relevant to the property. Scheduler fairness, delivery,
and cleanup assumptions must be named rather than excluded from the model by
assuming the desired callback order.

The proof need not claim that Chrome, the JavaScript engine, the operating
system, or the CPU is formally verified by Gesso.

Instead those primitives are explicit assumptions at the browser FFI or HTMX
integration boundary.

*** Execution isolation theorem

For distinct executions E1 and E2, an asynchronous event produced by a resource
owned exclusively by E1 must not alter:

- the Choreo state of E2;
- E2's provisional state;
- E2's target/resource ownership;
- E2's continuity-capture instance identity and ownership;
- an authoritative projection installed after E1 became stale;
- any other execution-scoped semantic state of E2.

Intentionally shared authoritative invalidation is not execution-private and is
handled through its own declared path.

This theorem should subsume broad families of race bugs involving:

- old settlement timers;
- old HTMX completions;
- old continuity callbacks;
- old fragment requests;
- target replacement followed by a new execution;
- duplicated external callbacks.

*** Authoritative installation prevents obsolete restoration

Once a logical target has installed an admitted authoritative observation that
supersedes provisional ownership for the relevant scope, cleanup or delayed
effects from the superseded execution must not restore presentation that violates
that observation or its ownership contract merely because a callback remains.

This prohibition does not impose a total ordering on observations or provenance,
nor prevent legitimate later domain changes. Any stronger monotonicity claim
must specify the ordering and scope for which it is established.

The implementation should rely on supported progression/ownership relations,
not arithmetic ordering of opaque tokens.

This property prevents:

#+begin_example
canonical authoritative response arrives
        |
old failure callback runs
        |
old optimistic snapshot is restored
#+end_example

The same rule applies when two authoritative acquisitions have different
scopes.  A response that is newer in one progression coordinate does not
automatically supersede every independently justified observation already
installed for the logical view.  Supersession is permitted only where the
declared scope, projection, ownership, and progression relations justify it.

*** Scoped authoritative installation and advisory discharge
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-runtime-scoped-authoritative-installation
:END:

The browser runtime must preserve the distinction between installing an
authoritative observation and claiming that an entire aggregate view has been
reacquired at one common snapshot.

An authoritative acquisition result must carry or resolve enough checked
semantic information for the runtime to determine the scope of what the result
actually establishes.  Depending on the existing Gesso realization, that may be
represented by projection identity, progression requirements, acquisition
metadata, semantic ownership, or another checked mechanism.  This requirement
does not mandate a universal per-object revision number or a new persisted
history structure.

For a scoped installation, the runtime must be able to preserve all of the
following simultaneously:

- a newly installed authoritative observation for the acquisition's covered
  object or projection;
- other independently justified authoritative observations whose scopes are not
  superseded by that installation;
- unrelated advisory obligations not discharged by the acquisition;
- local presentation state that remains compatible with the adopted semantic
  view;
- provisional state whose ownership has not been superseded or otherwise
  resolved by the installation.

Therefore:

#+begin_example
response completed
    !=
all advisory information discharged

newer database progression observed for object S
    !=
whole board reacquired at that progression
#+end_example

A completed acquisition may discharge only obligations satisfied under the
[[#req-advisory-discharge][Part IV advisory-discharge contract]]. An obligation
requiring adoption into a visible projection is not discharged solely because
the response was fetched or parsed. The runtime must distinguish justified
observation, admitted installation, physical realization, and completion of any
required incorporation step.
An advisory that arrives while a read is in flight remains outstanding when the
returned observation does not cover the later obligation.

Likewise, if a local correlated operation installs a newer justified
authoritative observation before an older Refresh response arrives, the older
response must not blindly replace the newer installation.  The runtime may
reject the older result, preserve independently compatible portions, schedule a
successor acquisition, or use another checked reconciliation strategy already
supported by Gesso.  It must not merge incompatible snapshots merely to avoid
another read.

**** Installation and physical realization are distinct stages

An admitted authoritative observation is not invalidated by a later DOM or
continuity failure. Nor does admission establish that its intended physical
representation was successfully installed.

The adapter must track enough status to distinguish the observation it has
accepted, the representation currently realized, and any outstanding rendering
or adoption obligation. A failed or partially completed installation must
follow a declared repair path rather than claim a coherent DOM that does not
exist. The affected presentation must not expose enabled semantic affordances
based on an incoherent mixture of bindings and content.

The integration need not promise one atomic transaction over arbitrary DOM
effects. It must specify the admitted realization boundary and failure handling
for the supported swap/effect set. It may preserve already compatible portions
or reacquire and reconstruct the affected scope where the contract permits it.
It must not re-enable obsolete provisional restoration to hide a rendering fault.

Continuity failure after successful authoritative installation is a local
presentation fault. It does not revoke that installation or the authoritative
model result, as specified in [[#req-continuity-boundary][Part V]].

*** Fragment coordination contract
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-fragment-coordinator
:END:

For each admitted logical coordination scope, the default supported coordinator
admits at most one active logical acquisition and one queued acquisition slot:

#+begin_example
active logical acquisition count <= 1
queued acquisition slot count    <= 1
#+end_example

The slot may contain a normalized bundle of scoped requirements. Its meaning
follows [[#req-progression-combination][Part IV's satisfaction and combination
contract]]. Slot count is not a proof that this bundle or all retained metadata
is bounded. A realization must state its supported scope domain, representation
bounds, and treatment of incomparable requirements.

Physical requests may outlive logical retirement. Therefore the logical bound
does not claim that only one physical request exists in the browser at every
instant. Physical cancellation debt and its operational limits are tracked
separately and cannot retain semantic installation rights.

**** Advisory debt is distinct from permission to acquire

Receiving an advisory records the scoped requirement. Whether to start an
acquisition depends on the declared application policy and current authorized
acquisition path. A pending advisory does not by itself create a queued command
to automatically adopt remote content.

An explicit Refresh may admit acquisition of a captured requirement under a
declared action scope. The contract must say whether that action also permits
successor reads needed for the same recovery, or whether a later advisory needs
another user action. A completion callback must not make that policy choice by
accident. The requirement cannot disappear merely because permission to acquire
it has not yet been supplied.

Acquisition permission is local scheduling/adoption policy, not server-side
authorization. Each protected read still follows the trusted authorization path.

**** Coordinator state and transitions

The state must represent, directly or through admitted references:

- logical coordination scope and current owner instance;
- active acquisition identity, captured requirement, and relevant policy context;
- outstanding requirements and any queued acquisition permitted to satisfy them;
- admitted observations and adopted projection state needed for comparison;
- physical-resource correlations and failure/recovery status relevant to safety.

The following transitions define the required behavior without prescribing one
public record layout:

| Event | Required semantic result |
|-------+--------------------------|
| Advisory arrives while idle | Retain its requirement; start only if declared policy permits acquisition |
| Advisory arrives while active | Preserve its requirement using safe combination; do not relabel it covered by the captured request |
| Refresh or other permitted trigger | Admit the requested acquisition scope and captured obligations; queue or start according to current ownership |
| Active response arrives | Validate correlation, authority/projection contract, scope, consistency, and installation compatibility before applying it |
| Response covers obligations | Discharge only the acquisition or incorporation obligations actually satisfied; retain the rest |
| Active read fails or is cancelled | End that logical attempt; preserve unsatisfied obligations and follow declared retry/recovery policy |
| Owner retires or is replaced | Revoke its private permits and correlations; explicitly retain, transfer, or re-establish still-required coordination state |
| Retired response arrives | Reject its obsolete installation path; do not infer ownership from the current target |

When an active attempt ends, at most one queued acquisition is admitted next,
and only if its policy and resource conditions still hold. Failed reads must
not create an uncontrolled immediate retry loop. Retry limits, delay, and
availability assumptions belong to the chosen recovery contract; stopping a
retry does not falsely discharge its requirement.

A successor response may independently satisfy an obligation that arrived after
the request began. In that case a redundant read is unnecessary, but coverage
must be established from the returned observation rather than assumed from
completion order.

**** Mechanical checking and concrete correspondence

The coordinator's formal or exact-finite claim must specify the abstract states,
events, coverage relations, policy choices, and retained-data abstraction used.
Current/stale ownership and covered/uncovered obligation relations can be useful
abstractions only when their soundness for the claimed property is justified.
A small sample of concrete tokens or two queue slots does not establish this.

The checked properties should include non-loss of obligations, absence of stale
installation, preservation of independent scopes, policy-correct acquisition,
and the stated logical concurrency bound. Progress claims additionally require
their scheduling, user-action, resource, and authoritative-availability premises.

*** Resource/terminal invariant

Every physical resource represented in the semantic adapter state has exactly
one declared semantic owner or explicitly shared ownership class while that
ownership is active.

Retirement first revokes the execution's semantic ownership and the admission
rights of its execution-private resource instances. After that
semantic retirement, the execution owns zero execution-private semantic
resources even if some corresponding physical browser objects have not yet been
successfully destroyed.

Physical cleanup is then idempotent, best effort, and diagnostic. A timer,
listener, HTMX request, continuity object, or other physical resource may outlive
semantic retirement because cancellation raced, threw, was ignored by a library,
or the browser was suspended. Such a leftover resource is cleanup debt, not a
source of semantic authority. Any later callback from it must be rejected by the
owner/resource-instance admission gate.

New semantic ownership therefore depends on successful revocation of old
semantic ownership, not on proof that every old physical resource has already
vanished.

The target theorem is:

#+begin_quote
Once execution E is semantically retired, no subsequently delivered private
event or deferred effect may use E's revoked ownership to alter current
execution or presentation state, regardless of whether E's physical cleanup
completed successfully. Independently admitted shared or command-resolution
events retain only the rights of their own declared owner.
#+end_quote

Cleanup failure in the physical shell is recorded as an external/runtime fault;
it must not mutate the semantic transition history into an impossible earlier
state and must not block safe logical reuse merely because physical cleanup is
incomplete.

*** Adversarial-browser authority theorem

The formal server-side protocol should be checked under an adversarial client
model rather than assuming the generated browser projection is honest.

The client may send any syntactically representable request bytes.

Subject to the named authentication/model/XTDB assumptions, arbitrary client
behavior must not be sufficient to:

- establish another principal identity;
- acquire an undeclared authority role;
- manufacture authoritative provenance;
- jump the trusted server projection to a privileged state;
- perform a model transition not permitted to the authenticated principal;
- obtain a protected authoritative observation or Live subscription that the
  authenticated principal is not authorized to receive.

This is stronger than proving that the generated JavaScript follows the global
choreography.

The honest projected browser is a usability/correctness implementation.
Security on the trusted side must remain valid even when that implementation is
replaced entirely.

*** Adapter state has one semantic owner

The browser adapter is the owner of browser coordination semantics and composes
the portable execution machines under the [[#req-portable-execution][Part V state
ownership contract]]. Each fact has one semantic owner. A portable execution
may own its trajectory while the adapter owns fragment coordination and the
conditions under which that execution is resumed.

HTMX, EventSource callbacks, timers, continuity code, and DOM helpers may
produce normalized observations or realize abstract effects.

They must not each keep a partial private copy of:

- which execution is current;
- which admitted execution or effect-scope owner holds the target;
- whether authority has superseded optimism;
- whether rollback remains legal;
- whether an event may resume a machine.

Duplicated ownership state is exactly how individually reasonable callbacks
produce impossible global behavior.

*** Semantic admission precedes physical action when intervention is required

Where Gesso must decide whether an HTMX lifecycle action is semantically
admissible, the order is:

#+begin_example
raw lifecycle event
    |
normalize
    |
semantic adapter decision
    |
allow / cancel / ignore / correlate
    |
HTMX continues or is suppressed
#+end_example

The shell must not first perform the action and ask the semantic machine
afterward whether it should have happened.

This does not require Gesso to control every ordinary HTMX lifecycle step.
Intervention is narrow and only where a Gesso invariant requires it.

*** HTMX remains declarative infrastructure rather than a hidden transport beneath Gesso

The architecture deliberately avoids rebuilding ordinary HTMX behavior.

For supported interactions, HTMX continues to own:

- request execution;
- normal response processing;
- swaps;
- out-of-band processing;
- settle behavior;
- its documented lifecycle.

Gesso owns the semantic constraints layered around that behavior.

This distinction is valuable because it keeps the trusted integration contract
small enough to document and test.

If a future HTMX version changes the lifecycle contract, the supported-contract
adapter may change.  Choreo/browser semantic invariants should not be rewritten
merely because an external library renamed events or changed implementation
mechanisms.

*** Supported HTMX behavior is a named assumption

The framework should define which externally documented HTMX behaviors it relies
on.

Anything outside that supported subset may be:

- unverified;
- rejected;
- explicitly unsupported;
- admitted only under a weaker stated guarantee.

The system must not silently claim Gesso's stronger browser invariants while
allowing configuration that bypasses the lifecycle gates on which those
invariants depend.

*** Browser FFI is intentionally dumb

The browser FFI may know how to:

- start/cancel a timer;
- install provisional DOM;
- capture/restore continuity;
- observe a raw event;
- ask HTMX to cancel an operation through a supported hook;
- install/remove a listener;
- report physical failure.

It should not know why a rollback is legal, why a resource instance is currently admitted, or why
a settlement wins.

Those semantic facts are outputs of the pure transition core.

This keeps the trusted correspondence boundary narrow and makes browser races
amenable to explicit state-transition reasoning.

*** Correlation is installed, then carried opaquely

When semantic logic creates or admits a physical resource, the resource receives
the identity needed to report back safely.

For example:

#+begin_example
adapter emits
    [:timer/start
      {:execution-id E
       :timer-id T
       :resource-instance I}]

shell installs timer carrying E/T/I

timer fires

shell reports
    {:event :timer/fired
     :execution-id E
     :timer-id T
     :resource-instance I}
#+end_example

The shell must not rediscover E/T/I later from:

- DOM ancestry;
- CSS selector;
- target contents;
- "currently active" global variables;
- URL shape.

Opaque installed correlation is more reliable than semantic reconstruction from
mutable browser state.

*** Stale and duplicate events are ordinary modeled cases

Asynchronous browsers naturally produce events that arrive after their semantic
usefulness has ended.

Examples:

- request completion after execution retirement;
- timer after settlement;
- continuity callback after target replacement;
- duplicate request completion;
- old fragment response after a newer authoritative install.

The runtime should treat these as explicit event classes admitted by the safety
model, not as impossible programming errors.

The ordinary result is often:

#+begin_example
recognized
classified stale/duplicate
semantic no-op
optional diagnostic record
#+end_example

This is stronger than hoping cancellation prevents delivery.

*** Cancellation is an optimization; invalidation is the safety mechanism

Physical cancellation can race or fail.

Therefore the safety argument may not depend on proving that every old:

- timer;
- request;
- listener;
- observer;
- callback;

was physically destroyed.

Semantic retirement revokes the affected owner or resource instance's semantic
admission rights.

After that point a late physical callback is powerless.

Successful physical cancellation remains desirable for efficiency and resource
use, but it is not the sole correctness gate.

*** Execution isolation is intentionally local except for declared shared events

Most asynchronous resources are execution-private.

Shared authoritative invalidation is different.

For example, an invalidation about a logical Request fragment may legitimately
matter to a newer execution even if it was triggered by another operation.

The runtime therefore distinguishes:

#+begin_example
execution-private callback
    -> must satisfy owner/resource-instance gate

declared shared authoritative invalidation
    -> follows fragment/progression coordination path
#+end_example

Treating all events as either globally shared or strictly private would be
incorrect.

*** Fragment refresh uses the declared coordinator contract

Invalidations can arrive faster than authoritative reads complete. They must
enter the [[#req-fragment-coordinator][fragment coordinator]] instead of starting an
independent request per advisory. That contract defines the logical request
bound, preserved scoped obligations, acquisition policy, and the evidence needed
for any finite-state or retained-memory claim. It is the single normative owner
of those coordination rules.

*** Authoritative installation revokes weaker obsolete restoration rights

A stale failure callback may still know an old optimistic snapshot.

That fact does not mean restoring the snapshot remains legal.

After authoritative installation, older provisional cleanup may:

- release resources;
- record diagnostics;
- retire execution state.

It may not reinstall weaker/older representation merely because its callback
finally ran.

This is prevention of obsolete restoration after authoritative installation.

The comparison is semantic, based on authority/ownership/progression relations,
not numeric arithmetic over opaque basis tokens.

*** Semantic retirement precedes physical cleanup

Retirement has a precise order:

#+begin_example
1. mark execution semantically retired
2. revoke admission rights of execution-private resource instances
3. release semantic ownership
4. permit new semantic owner where valid
5. attempt physical cleanup best effort
#+end_example

This order matters.

If physical cleanup is required before semantic retirement, one uncancellable
timer or third-party request can prevent logical progress forever.

If semantic ownership remains until cleanup finishes, stale callbacks may retain
power they should already have lost.

*** Browser security assumes arbitrary client bytes

The trusted server may not rely on:

- the generated adapter having run;
- local plan state being honest;
- execution/resource-instance correlation being truthful;
- hidden inputs being untouched;
- client role labels being valid;
- a client-provided nominal tag, record name, wrapper, or discriminator having
  crossed the trusted construction path it claims to represent;
- a button having existed;
- a sidecar claiming verification.

The server validates syntax/protocol structure as needed, then derives
authenticated principal and current permission from trusted state and model
semantics.

This adversarial model is part of the browser architecture, not an optional
security-hardening mode.

** Projected-plan identity, deployment, and stale continuation recovery
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-stale-plan-recovery
:END:

A projected choreography may remain in a browser across deployment.

Old or suspended browsers must not be assumed compatible with a newly deployed
server forever.

The design therefore distinguishes:

- choreography representation version;
- projected-plan version;
- semantic operation identity/version where needed;
- wire/protocol version;
- semantic command identity;
- protocol execution identity;
- application/server compatibility identity where needed;
- admitted assembly generation identity where required for realization binding.

Deployment identity records placement or rollout. It is not interchangeable with
assembly, execution, resource-instance, or compatibility identity.

These do not need to become one global version integer.

A compatibility check should answer whether a suspended or retried projection is
safe to continue with the current server/runtime contract.

For example:

#+begin_example
browser loaded projected plan P7
        |
browser sleeps
        |
deployment introduces incompatible P8/server contract
        |
browser wakes
#+end_example

The safe default is:

#+begin_example
detect incompatible continuation
        |
semantically retire stale execution
and revoke its execution-private callback rights
        |
initiate best-effort physical cleanup
        |
re-establish transport/authentication
        |
authoritative reread/reload
        |
instantiate current projection
#+end_example

Instantiation of the new projection must not depend on successful destruction
of every stale physical browser resource. Once semantic retirement and ownership
revocation has made stale callbacks powerless, physical cleanup may complete
later or fail diagnostically without restoring the old execution's ownership.

The framework should not invent transparent migration of arbitrary suspended
machine state unless an explicit migration facility is designed and tested.

Compatibility failure is a recovery condition, not evidence that authoritative
model state is invalid or that a pending command was rejected. Recovery must
preserve the distinction between current-state reconstruction and attribution
of an earlier command, following [[#req-command-participant-uncertainty][Part IV]].

Changing authenticated principal or protected view context is also an admission
boundary. Old pending bindings, subscriptions, or observations must not be
silently treated as belonging to the new principal. The runtime must retire or
revalidate the affected scopes under the current contract; the server remains
responsible for every protected read and mutation.

This is particularly important for low-end Android phones whose tabs may survive
sleep, backgrounding, or long network outages across deployments.

*** Stale plan recovery protects authoritative truth, not suspended local illusion

When a browser wakes with an incompatible plan, the goal is not to preserve every
detail of its suspended local machine.

The goal is to recover safely to current authoritative truth.

The default recovery sequence is therefore intentionally destructive of stale
local execution state:

#+begin_example
detect incompatibility
    |
revoke stale semantic ownership
    |
revoke stale resource-instance admission rights
    |
best-effort cleanup
    |
re-establish current transport/authentication
    |
authoritative reacquisition
    |
instantiate current projection
#+end_example

A stale optimistic or continuity state may be lost.

Authoritative model truth is not invalid merely because the old projection can
no longer continue.

*** Compatibility is explicit and scoped

Compatibility may concern different dimensions:

- executable plan format;
- protocol envelope;
- operation meaning/version;
- settlement interpretation;
- progression representation;
- browser/server runtime contract.

The framework should not collapse these into one universal monotonically
increasing version integer unless a particular layer genuinely has such a
coordinate.

A compatibility predicate may therefore be more structured than equality.

What matters is that continuation is admitted only when the current runtime can
justify it.

*** Parseability is not compatibility

A new server may still understand the bytes of an old command while no longer
supporting the old continuation semantics.

Thus:

#+begin_example
can decode
    !=
safe to continue
#+end_example

The compatibility check occurs at the semantic/protocol boundary, not merely at
deserialization.

*** Transparent arbitrary state migration is a non-goal

Automatically translating arbitrary suspended projected-machine state across
deployments would require:

- source/target semantic correspondence;
- state migration rules;
- resource ownership migration;
- pending effect migration;
- timer/callback treatment;
- provisional state treatment;
- proof of compatibility.

The framework must not accidentally promise this by attempting heuristic
migration.

Specific cross-version migrations may be defined for high-value cases only when their compatibility semantics are explicit.

The safe baseline is authoritative reacquisition.

*** Same command may survive when execution cannot

A semantic command and its current projected execution have different identity.

An old execution may be retired while the application still recognizes the same
semantic command for reconciliation or retry.

For example:

#+begin_example
command C42 persists semantically

old:
    plan P7
    execution E7

deployment

new:
    plan P8
    execution E8

C42 may be reconciled/retried if operation policy permits
#+end_example

This is one reason command identity, execution identity, and plan identity must
remain separate.

*** Recovery must itself obey authorization and progression rules

Authoritative reacquisition after stale-plan detection is not a privileged
backdoor.

The new read/subscription path must still:

- bind the authenticated principal;
- authorize protected reads;
- satisfy required progression semantics;
- install only current compatible projections.

Recovery changes runtime topology, not security policy. It also does not waive
explicit adoption policy: reconstructing a board after reconnect must follow
the declared policy for its existing observations and remote membership. If a
full reload intentionally establishes a new initial observation, that is a
separately declared acquisition boundary rather than a hidden advisory-triggered
adoption.

Recovery must handle failure to acquire a compatible plan or authoritative read
without repeatedly restarting an incompatible continuation. The unavailable or
retry state and the conditions for another attempt must be explicit. A missing
current artifact is not permission to execute the stale plan.

*** Offline duration does not imply replay obligation

A browser offline through many authoritative changes need not replay every
intermediate invalidation or projection.

If application semantics permit it, recovery may jump directly to a sufficiently
current authoritative observation.

This keeps Live advisory and convergence semantics compatible with very long
sleep/offline periods.

** Browser managed effects
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-browser-managed-effects
:END:

A choreography execution may cause browser implementation effects with
lifetimes longer than one pure machine step.

These must be owned explicitly.

Semantic ownership decisions belong to the pure browser adapter transition core.
The physical browser effect shell may allocate/cancel concrete resources only in
response to abstract effects emitted by that core; it must not maintain a second
competing semantic ownership model.

No supported execution or shared browser activity may create ongoing managed
browser resources without registering their declared owner with the runtime.
This requirement concerns Gesso-managed effects, not every unrelated browser
resource created by the surrounding application.

Effect categories may include:

- *resource* -- must be released;
- *reversible* -- may be compensated while still safe;
- *authoritative* -- records installed canonical state and must never be
  automatically rolled back merely because later cleanup failed;
- *async* -- timers/callback sources that must be cancelled or invalidated;
- *ephemeral* -- recorded for diagnostics but leaves no ongoing resource.

An execution should have one managed effect scope.

Examples of owned browser effects:

- optimistic target lock;
- settlement timer;
- request correlation;
- continuity slot/transaction;
- provisional DOM state;
- pending browser state;
- callback/event subscriptions associated with the execution.

Important invariants include:

#+begin_example
every active managed effect scope has an admitted execution or shared owner
every active execution's managed effects belong to its declared effect scope
every managed timer/lock/continuity object has declared owner and instance identity
retired execution retains no execution-private semantic ownership or callback authority
physical remnants after retirement are cleanup debt only
#+end_example

Catastrophic cleanup should be:

- reverse acquisition order where required by the declared resource dependencies;
- idempotent;
- best effort;
- continuing through remaining permitted cleanup actions even when one throws;
- diagnostic about cleanup failures;
- subordinate to the original failure;
- forbidden from undoing already-established authoritative DOM merely because a
  later cleanup failed.

Stale-plan retirement uses the same ownership rules: an incompatible old
execution must first lose semantic ownership and its execution-private callback
rights.
A new projection may then assume the logical target without waiting for every
old physical effect to be destroyed. Cleanup of those stale physical effects is
best effort, and any callbacks they later emit remain powerless because they
carry retired execution or resource-instance correlation.

*** One effect scope per active execution

An active projected execution should have one semantic effect scope that records
the resources and reversible/provisional obligations it owns.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
Execution E
    |
    +--> target ownership
    +--> provisional projection
    +--> settlement timer
    +--> request correlation
    +--> continuity slot
    +--> subscriptions/callback registrations
#+end_example

The exact representation may be compact.

The important property is that resource ownership is explicit and queryable by
the semantic transition system.

*** Effect category affects legal cleanup

Not every effect supports the same compensation.

A useful conceptual distinction is:

- resource -- requires release/invalidation;
- reversible -- may be compensated while the compensation remains semantically
  legal;
- authoritative -- must never be undone by local cleanup merely because later
  delivery failed;
- async -- creates future callbacks whose semantic admission rights must be
  revocable independently of best-effort physical cancellation;
- ephemeral -- has no ongoing semantic ownership.

An implementation may encode categories differently.

What matters is that cleanup logic cannot blindly run "undo" on every historical
effect.

*** Rollback rights expire

An optimistic execution may initially own the right to restore a captured local
snapshot if its request fails before stronger authority supersedes it.

That right is conditional.

It expires when, for example:

- a newer authoritative projection installs;
- another current execution legitimately owns the target;
- the relevant execution or resource instance is retired;
- reconciliation chooses a different canonical representation.

Therefore rollback is a semantic operation guarded by current ownership, not a
generic exception handler.

*** Catastrophic cleanup preserves the original semantic failure

If cleanup action 1 throws, cleanup actions 2 through N should still be
attempted.

Cleanup exceptions are diagnostics subordinate to the original failure that
triggered cleanup.

A later cleanup error must not rewrite history so the system appears to have
failed for a different semantic reason.

Conceptually:

#+begin_example
primary failure F
    |
semantic retirement
    |
cleanup A  -> fails CA
cleanup B  -> succeeds
cleanup C  -> fails CC

reported:
    primary F
    cleanup diagnostics [CA CC]
#+end_example

*** Cleanup is idempotent

The runtime should tolerate repeated cleanup attempts because:

- timeout and request failure may race;
- browser lifecycle recovery may repeat retirement;
- exception handling may retry cleanup;
- physical cancellation may be uncertain.

Idempotent physical cleanup plus owner/resource-instance admission safety is preferable
to fragile exactly-once cleanup assumptions.

*** Physical remnants are observable debt, not hidden success

When cleanup cannot destroy a resource, the runtime should retain enough
diagnostic information to explain the remnant.

It should not silently claim complete physical cleanup. Semantic isolation does
not prove a bound on physical leaks. The operational contract must define how
retained diagnostics and remnant resources are bounded, reclaimed, or reported
as an unrecoverable local resource condition where repeated failure would
otherwise exhaust the browser. A resource limit may suspend new work through a
declared unavailable state; it must not revive obsolete ownership.

At the same time, the semantic system may proceed once the resource's callback
authority has been revoked.

This separation lets the framework remain safe under imperfect browsers and
third-party runtimes without concealing operational problems.

*** Shared resources require an explicit shared ownership class

The "one owner" rule applies to execution-private resources.

Some resources may be intentionally shared, for example a fragment-level
coordinator or shared Live connection.

Such sharing must be represented explicitly.

An unowned global variable that several executions happen to mutate is not a
shared ownership model.

The semantic design needs to know the class of sharing well enough to prevent an
execution's retirement from destroying resources still required by legitimate
owners.

** Async event safety
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-browser-async-admission
:END:

Every asynchronous event delivery must first be normalized at the browser FFI
boundary and then pass the semantic adapter safety gate. There must not be an
alternate callback path that directly mutates execution state or resumes the
portable machine.

The common gate follows [[#req-owner-relative-event-admission][Part II's
owner-relative admission rule]]. For an execution-private event that resumes a
projected machine, the runtime must establish:

- the execution still exists;
- correlation identifies the admitted execution instance and the concrete
  resource instance whose event may resume it;
- the resource that produced the event is still owned;
- the event is accepted in the machine's current state;
- where relevant, the projected plan is still compatible with the runtime/server
  contract.

Otherwise the event is stale, duplicate, or incompatible and is ignored,
converted into recovery, or recorded diagnostically.

Retirement forbids resuming the retired owner; it does not prove that the
associated command failed or that every later authoritative result is useless.
Command-outcome or authoritative evidence may enter through an independently
declared admission path without reactivating the old execution or granting its
callbacks permission to mutate replacement state. Normalization cannot invent
that alternate path by relabeling a rejected callback as a shared event.

All of the following event sources use the common admission boundary, with
the checks required by their declared ownership class:

- timers;
- HTMX lifecycle callbacks;
- request failures;
- settlements;
- Live/canonical supersession;
- continuity callbacks;
- fragment request completions;
- Refresh/authoritative-acquisition completions and their captured requirements;
- browser lifecycle recovery;
- other asynchronous browser events.

If machine/FX execution throws unexpectedly:

- mark the execution failed;
- never restore an old pre-drive machine as though the failed transition did not
  occur;
- revoke the failed owner's semantic permits and retire the affected execution
  or admitted coordination scope before attempting physical cleanup;
- attempt managed cleanup without reviving obsolete ownership;
- retain failure history and cleanup diagnostics;
- report/rethrow the original error.

When settlement or authoritative supersession wins, settlement timeout ownership
should be cancelled immediately before slow/local rendering work such as
continuity restoration.

*** One admission gate for every asynchronous callback

The browser must not have one safety gate for timers, another informal rule for
HTMX, and a third special case for continuity.

Every asynchronous callback that can influence Gesso semantic state passes
through the same conceptual admission sequence:

#+begin_example
normalize callback
    |
resolve declared event class, owner, and correlation
    |
owner admitted and applicable?
    |
resource instance / execution identity current where required?
    |
resource still semantically owned under that class?
    |
event legal in the owning execution or coordinator state?
    |
scope / progression / plan compatibility satisfied where required?
    |
accept transition
#+end_example

Any failure before the final step means the callback cannot directly perform
the proposed semantic mutation. The gate may emit the specified diagnostic or
recovery transition. It must not redispatch a rejected private event as shared
to bypass the failed check.

An idle fragment may accept an admitted shared advisory with no command
execution. A stale subscription instance may not impersonate the replacement.
An old settlement may inform a current command-resolution process only through
its separately admitted command-evidence path; it does not resume the retired
execution whose private callback was rejected.

*** Unknown, stale, duplicate, and incompatible are distinct diagnoses

All may result in "do not resume the machine," but they describe different
conditions.

- unknown -- no recognized owner/resource correlation exists;
- stale -- correlation refers to a retired execution or replaced/retired
  resource instance;
- duplicate -- equivalent event was already consumed;
- incompatible -- continuation exists but current plan/runtime contract forbids
  it;
- illegal-state -- event belongs to an admitted owner but is not admissible from
  that owner's current execution or coordination state.

Keeping these classifications separate improves diagnostics and testing.

*** Late callback safety must not rely on ordering luck

JavaScript event scheduling, network completion, HTMX lifecycle ordering, and
browser suspension make many orderings possible.

The design must therefore be correct for adversarial scheduling within the
documented external contracts.

Tests should deliberately permute:

- response versus timeout;
- canonical install versus old request failure;
- execution retirement versus continuity completion;
- invalidation versus fragment response;
- advisory arrival during an in-flight Refresh;
- newer local authoritative installation versus an older Refresh response;
- reconnect versus old callback;
- duplicate external events;
- deferred physical effect versus owner retirement;
- shared advisory arrival while no command execution exists;
- explicit Refresh permission versus later advisory arrival;
- partial installation failure versus continuity completion;
- authentication-context replacement versus old subscription or request events.

The proof/model abstraction should classify events by semantically relevant
relations such as admitted/retired owner and current/replaced resource instance,
rather than a few chosen concrete integers. A resource can be replaced without
retiring its execution, and different owners can reuse a logical target without
sharing admission rights. A sound abstraction must preserve these distinctions
where they affect the property.

*** Unexpected semantic-machine failure retires rather than rewinds

If the semantic machine throws while driving a transition, the runtime must not
restore an earlier machine object and pretend no transition was attempted.

The safe response is:

#+begin_example
record primary failure
    |
retire affected semantic owner / revoke effect permits
    |
invalidate private resource correlations
    |
cleanup physical resources best effort
    |
surface original failure
#+end_example

Rewinding arbitrary partial semantic execution would require explicit
transactional semantics that the browser machine does not claim to provide.
The runtime may retain the last committed state for diagnosis and recovery, but
must not use it to replay possibly issued effects without the effect contract's
duplicate-handling guarantees. A failure after effect dispatch must account for
the possibility that physical work already occurred.

*** Winning terminal events cancel competing timeout authority immediately

When a settlement or authoritative supersession wins, any settlement timeout
must lose semantic ownership before slower local presentation work runs.

For example:

#+begin_example
settlement accepted
    |
cancel/invalidate timeout ownership
    |
then perform continuity / DOM follow-up
#+end_example

Otherwise a timeout may fire during a slow local effect and incorrectly compete
with an already resolved semantic outcome.

*** Duplicate delivery is part of the protocol environment

External systems may duplicate:

- SSE messages;
- request completions;
- browser events;
- timer observations after cancellation races.

Where a duplicate is semantically harmless, the transition system should make
that idempotence explicit.

Where it is not harmless, correlation/state must make the second delivery
rejectable.

"Should only happen once" is not a sufficient browser invariant.

** Authoritative absence
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-authoritative-absence
:END:

The runtime must be able to represent that an authoritative result establishes
absence of a previously rendered target within a declared projection scope.
That does not automatically establish nonexistence of the domain entity.

A missing canonical target is not always an error.

For example, a Request may legitimately disappear from a filtered board because
it became terminal or ceased to match the current view.

The semantic protocol therefore needs a notion equivalent to authoritative
absence/tombstone for projections.

The browser realization may then remove the corresponding DOM safely while
preserving the choreography's authority semantics.

An actor can acquire authoritative knowledge of absence in the same sense that
it can acquire authoritative knowledge of presence.

*** Presence and absence are both authoritative projection outcomes

A projection result should distinguish:

#+begin_example
present authoritative value
authoritative absence
not yet acquired
failed to acquire
#+end_example

These are not equivalent.

In particular:

#+begin_example
empty DOM
#+end_example

does not by itself tell the runtime whether the authoritative target is absent
or simply has not been loaded.

This distinction is necessary for both initial acquisition and safe removal.

*** Authoritative absence may legitimately remove local representation

Examples include a Request that:

- becomes terminal and is filtered out;
- moves to another board scope;
- no longer satisfies the current search/filter projection;
- is deleted/withdrawn under domain policy where such absence is authoritative.

The browser may remove the representation when the authoritative projection
establishes absence.

That removal has the same authority semantics as installing an authoritative
present representation.

*** Absence supersedes obsolete provisional presence

If a provisional execution rendered a target and authority later establishes
that the target is absent from the relevant projection, the provisional target
must not survive merely because it has a physical DOM node.

Likewise a stale rollback cannot resurrect the target after authoritative
absence has been installed.

This is another instance of prevention of obsolete restoration after authoritative installation.

*** Absence is scoped to a projection

Authoritative absence from one projection does not necessarily mean the domain
entity ceased to exist.

For example:

#+begin_example
Request R absent from
    "open requests visible to this helper"

does not imply

Request R does not exist
#+end_example

The provenance of absence therefore includes the authoritative projection/read
whose result established it, including the completeness and consistency needed
to justify the negative result. Access denial, a partial page, a failed request,
or missing HTML is not interchangeable with authoritative absence.

Local presentation filtering is also distinct. Hiding a terminal card using an
option over already-adopted data does not necessarily perform a new acquisition
or revoke adopted membership. Re-showing it must use retained justified content
or a permitted acquisition, not resurrect a target whose authoritative absence
or ownership supersession has since been established. It must not admit unrelated
remote membership merely because a filter changed.

This keeps filtering/view semantics separate from entity existence semantics.

** Failure, liveness, and convergence

Choreography verification must distinguish safety from liveness.

A safety property might say:

#+begin_quote
Every reachable machine state is allowed by the global choreography.
#+end_quote

A liveness property might say:

#+begin_quote
Under eventual delivery, successful authoritative observation, and continued
execution, every provisional claim eventually reaches a resolving outcome.
#+end_quote

The verifier must not claim unconditional termination merely because every
reachable state has some path to a terminal state.

Liveness guarantees depend on explicit assumptions such as:

- message/event delivery where communication is required;
- authoritative dependencies remaining reachable;
- authoritative rereads eventually completing where observation is required;
- participants continuing to execute;
- clocks/timers firing;
- browser/runtime compatibility or successful recovery after incompatibility;
- absence of permanent unresolved application-defined conflict.

These assumptions should be named rather than hidden.

A continuously changing universe may prevent all actors from simultaneously
observing the latest state.

The correctness target is that actor knowledge remains valid relative to its
known basis and provenance and that relevant actors have a finite path toward
newer authoritative knowledge.

For HumanHelp, replay of every intermediate invalidation is not required for
convergence.

A client that was offline from X17 through X900 may recover by obtaining a
current authoritative observation at X900 or later rather than replaying every
intermediate change.

*** Safety, liveness, and recovery are separate claims

A safe protocol may still wait forever if an external dependency never responds.

A live protocol under favorable assumptions may still be unsafe if it can enter
an invalid state before eventual recovery.

The design therefore reports these classes separately.

Representative safety claim:

#+begin_example
stale execution callback can never mutate newer execution state
#+end_example

Representative conditional liveness claim:

#+begin_example
if authority remains reachable and the actor continues executing,
every provisional command eventually reaches authoritative resolution
or a declared recovery terminal
#+end_example

The assumptions belong in the claim.

*** Path-to-terminal is not termination proof

Finding a terminal path from every state does not prove the environment will
take it.

For example, a machine may have:

#+begin_example
waiting
    -- settlement --> confirmed
    -- timeout ----> local recovery / outcome still unknown
#+end_example

but if neither settlement nor timer delivery is assumed eventually to occur,
unconditional termination has not been established.

The verifier must not promote graph reachability into liveness.

*** Agreement debt may converge under quiescence

Actors can legitimately hold different authoritative frontiers while the world
changes.

Where the application requires convergence, the system should make the debt
visible and provide a finite route toward newer authoritative knowledge.

Under suitable assumptions such as:

- relevant authority eventually stops changing long enough;
- required communication/invalidations are eventually delivered or replaced by
  authoritative reacquisition;
- actors continue running;
- authoritative reads succeed;
- any user/application acquisition trigger required by policy occurs;

agreement debt should be able to fall to zero for the scoped facts that require
agreement.

This is a conditional convergence statement, not a claim of instantaneous
global consistency.

*** Replay of every intermediate change is usually unnecessary

A browser that slept through:

#+begin_example
X17 ... X900
#+end_example

may often recover directly by authoritative observation at X900 or later.

This is especially appropriate when Live invalidations are advisory rather than
durable semantic events.

The system needs current justified knowledge, not a reenactment of every
notification packet.

Applications whose domain semantics genuinely require every event must model a
durable event/log contract explicitly instead of assuming SSE replay.

*** Failure classes preserve authoritative commit truth

Failures should remain classified according to where they occur.

At minimum:

#+begin_example
failure established before commit submission, or confirmed rejection of attempt
    this attempt did not commit its proposed transition

commit submitted but result unavailable
    outcome unknown to this participant

authoritative commit succeeded
then propagation failed
    mutation occurred
    delivery/recovery debt remains

browser local effect failed
    authoritative truth unchanged
    local execution may recover/retire

stale/incompatible execution detected
    authoritative truth remains valid
    local projection reacquires
#+end_example

Collapsing these categories into a generic "operation failed" destroys the
information needed for safe recovery.

These categories describe authoritative reality when that reality is actually
known at the observing boundary.  They must not be inferred solely from a
participant's transport symptom.  A timeout, connection loss, or missing
response may leave that participant unable to establish whether authoritative
commit occurred.  The command/optimism layer's participant-relative uncertainty
therefore remains in force here: runtime classification must not turn unknown
commit disposition into either confirmed rejection or confirmed incorporation.

*** Recovery must not fabricate success

A recovery path may:

- reread authority;
- supersede provisional state;
- instantiate a current plan;
- retry publication;
- reconcile a semantic command.

It must not report success for an authoritative transition that did not occur.

Similarly it must not report authoritative failure merely because local display
or notification failed after commit.

When command disposition remains unresolved, recovery may restore a correct
authoritative presentation without claiming that the participant has learned
which command produced that state.  Presentation convergence, command
attribution, and authoritative command disposition remain separate conclusions.

*** Runtime fault and semantic outcome remain different

A model may legitimately return semantic outcomes such as:

- rejected;
- already incorporated;
- conflict/reconciled;
- no longer applicable.

Those are not runtime failures.

Conversely:

- broken HTMX contract;
- malformed response envelope;
- browser FFI exception;
- impossible adapter state;
- unsupported plan version;

are runtime/integration failures, not domain outcomes.

Diagnostics and tests should keep the distinction explicit.

*** Convergence does not require automatic UI adoption

An actor may learn that newer authority exists without immediately adopting that
projection into visible UI.

HumanHelp deliberately uses this distinction for remote creation. Its policy
for remote lifecycle changes to already-admitted Requests remains the explicit
product decision recorded in the HumanHelp part; generic browser semantics do
not choose that policy.

Therefore a convergence path may include:

#+begin_example
advisory knowledge of newer authority
    |
user/application chooses refresh
    |
authoritative reacquisition
    |
visible adoption
#+end_example

The system can remain semantically correct during the interval as long as the
actor does not perform behavior requiring knowledge it has not adopted and the
application exposes the required path toward current authority.

Adoption remains scoped.  Installing one newer authoritative observation does
not itself admit unrelated remote membership changes, clear unrelated advisory
debt, or establish that every displayed object belongs to one common snapshot.
A Refresh indication may clear only when the authoritative acquisition actually
satisfies the obligations whose presence caused that indication.

*** Runtime/browser guarantees are layered

The strongest useful statement combines several assurance kinds:

#+begin_example
proved / mechanically checked:
    portable transition invariants
    execution isolation
    execution/resource-instance ownership invariants
    explicitly stated logical coordination bounds and requirement preservation
    prevention of obsolete restoration after authoritative installation
    projection/refinement properties for supported subset

runtime-enforced premises:
    event correlation
    owner/resource-instance admission checks
    plan compatibility
    schema validation
    progression decoding

trusted/external assumptions:
    browser primitives
    supported HTMX lifecycle
    scheduler/network liveness where claimed
    authentication/model/XTDB contracts
#+end_example

Real Chromium testing remains essential because it challenges the bindings and
external assumptions that the pure semantic model deliberately does not prove.

*** Browser realization invariant

At the end of the portable/browser runtime layer, the unified design requires:

#+begin_quote
Production executes one canonical projected semantic plan through a pure
portable transition core.  Browser integrations normalize observations and
realize abstract effects without independently deciding semantics. Every
asynchronous event is admitted according to its declared owner, resource
instance, correlation, legal state, and applicable scope/compatibility checks.
Shared events do not require an unrelated execution. Deferred physical effects
cannot use expired permits to mutate a newer representation.  Semantic retirement makes stale physical
resources powerless before best-effort cleanup.  New authoritative
installations cannot be undone by obsolete provisional callbacks, and scoped
authoritative installation discharges only advisory obligations actually covered
by its acquisition.  Older Refresh responses cannot erase newer justified state
or silently convert a mixed authoritative view into a fictitious common
snapshot.  Transport/runtime failure does not invent command rejection or
incorporation when disposition remains unknown.  Incompatible stale plans
recover through authoritative reacquisition rather than heuristic state
migration.  Proof/diagnostic artifacts may explain execution but can never
affect it, and liveness/convergence claims remain explicitly conditional on
their named environment assumptions.
#+end_quote
