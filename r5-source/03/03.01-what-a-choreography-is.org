This part defines choreography as a global semantic specification, the operation
contracts it exposes, and the obligations for deriving role-local behavior. It
uses the evidence classifications of Part I and the identity, admission,
knowledge, and authority model of Part II. Concrete model/FX integration follows
in Part IV; browser realization and whole-application construction follow in
Parts VI and VII.

The intended architecture supports ordinary full-language Clojure under the
[[#req-full-language-construction][checked construction contract]]. A small
portable choreography vocabulary is an interface for distributed coordination,
not a replacement for the application's implementation language. Opaque code
retains its named contracts and accepted premises through verification,
projection, assembly, and execution.

Examples use illustrative declaration and API names. They specify required
semantics, not a promise that the displayed syntax is an existing executable
API. Generated plan excerpts and state tables are inspection views, not a second
handwritten workflow. Fixture symbols such as R, C, and E denote identities or
values bound by the surrounding example.

** What a choreography is

A choreography is a global description of a distributed state machine involving
N roles.

The global choreography is projected into N role-local machines. At runtime,
concrete actors instantiate those roles and execute the local machines
independently. The entry/participation contract binds each role instance to its
actual actor and principal or authority. A role name is not a credential, and
two tabs for one principal do not share knowledge merely because that principal
is the same.

The application may have many concurrent executions of the same choreography.
Admission states which instances may participate, how their messages and
resources are separated, and whether a logical role can have multiple members.
An aggregate role is not silently treated as one omniscient actor. Projection
of one execution also does not prove isolation from other operations; model
invariants and atomic guards govern their shared authoritative effects.

There does not need to be a second centralized global machine executing beside
them. The global choreography is the specification from which local machines
are verified and projected.

Under the declared entry, runtime, and environmental premises, the interaction
of admitted projected machines must refine behavior allowed by the global
choreography. The refinement claim must identify its supported language subset,
observable behaviors, and treatment of internal or physical steps.

The refinement relation is between admitted global semantic executions and
admitted distributed executions of the independently projected machines under
the specified environment, message-delivery, and fault contracts. A global
=:communicate= occurrence is a semantic communication boundary; emission of a
packet by a projected sender is not, by itself, proof that the receiver admitted
the matching message or acquired its declared knowledge. Permitted hidden local
steps and transport activity require an explicit trace-observation relation.

A successful structural projection check, role-local reachability result, or
replay of one concrete schedule establishes only its stated checking property.
It is not an all-schedules refinement theorem or a fairness/liveness proof.
A stronger claim must quantify over the admitted schedules and retain its
reordering, delivery, terminal-outcome, and environment premises. In
particular, a projected role finishing does not mean it knows the authored
global terminal result unless the choreography establishes that knowledge.

This claim about cooperating projected machines does not assume that an
adversarial browser follows its projection. Trusted-side admission and model
enforcement must still preserve the protected authority boundary for arbitrary
client inputs, as specified in [[#req-trust-boundary][the trust model]].

A choreography may describe:

- local computation;
- communication;
- authoritative establishment and observation requirements;
- environmental events;
- branching;
- authoritative operations;
- provisional projections;
- resource acquisition and release;
- failure paths;
- timeout paths;
- settlement and reconciliation;
- convergence requirements.

A choreography does not imply that all participants change state atomically.
Distributed actors may temporarily hold different knowledge. Model guards
protect authoritative effects at their own qualified transition boundaries;
projection neither serializes every concurrent execution nor transfers one
participant's unobserved authoritative result to another. Missing messages,
indeterminate outcomes, and stalled environments remain explicit recovery
or progress obligations rather than being erased by successful construction.

The important distinction is between:

#+begin_example
authoritative state
provisional state
actor-local knowledge
#+end_example

and not a false claim that the entire distributed system changes simultaneously.

A choreography also does not imply one persistent runtime machine per connected
client or one persistent server process per conceptual workflow.

The global description may be realized by short-lived projections that interact
through durable authoritative facts.
