** Semantic evidence vocabulary

The following vocabulary is shared by the later compositional hardening and
whole-application proof architecture.

It is defined here so later layers do not need to redefine what their evidence
means.

*** Identity and instance terminology

An identity names a particular semantic object or concrete instance. Its meaning
must be explicit wherever admission, ownership, compatibility, or evidence
binding depends on it. The canonical distinctions include:

- *Command identity:* the logical intent, which may survive multiple attempts.
- *Execution identity:* one concrete semantic execution of a choreography or
  operation; its relation to a command is explicitly declared.
- *Resource-instance identity:* one concrete timer, subscription, transport
  request, continuity capture, or other managed resource, distinct from its
  reusable logical resource/target name.
- *Transport-request identity:* one transport attempt, distinct from the domain
  object being acted upon and from the command it may carry.
- *Submission-attempt identity:* one attempt to incorporate a command at an
  authoritative boundary; one transport request need not imply exactly one
  backend attempt.
- *Capability-instance identity:* a concrete grant to perform a scoped action
  under its lifetime and usage contract; it is not the command identifier.
- *Admitted assembly generation:* the immutable application realization admitted
  together, including its routes, providers, prepared operations, and artifacts.
- *Compatibility identity/version:* the declared protocol or plan compatibility
  class, distinct from exact executable-artifact identity and from deployment.

A deployment may host multiple admitted assembly generations during a supported
transition. A deployment identifier, assembly generation, execution identity,
and resource-instance identity are not interchangeable coordinates.

Use =generation= only when its owning subject and purpose are explicit, such as
=assembly generation= or a declared =resource-instance generation counter=.
A counter is one possible representation; opaque identity is sufficient where
only equality and non-aliasing are needed. Do not imply an ordering, universal
counter, or shared lifetime merely by calling a value a generation. A later
reference may abbreviate a locally established subject only when it remains
unambiguous.

For browser transport examples, =:transport-request-id= names the transport
attempt. Domain Request arguments such as HumanHelp's =:request-id= retain their
domain meaning. Different transport attempts must not be conflated because they
carry the same command or concern the same domain Request.

Operation identity is *preserved* through realization: it cannot be silently
erased or substituted. Reserve *monotonic* for a stated ordering or information-
growth relation. In particular, preventing retired work from restoring obsolete
presentation does not imply that all observations form one totally ordered
history or that every valid domain transition increases a numeric value.

*** Executable semantic core, proof kernel, and dedicated checkers

The *executable semantic core* defines the production transition meaning for its
supported semantic subset. It consumes the applicable executable rules, admitted
state, and inputs/events, and yields semantic state and declared effects. A
portable machine step or browser adapter transition function is an executable
semantic core within its stated scope. There need not be one universal step
function for all layers.

The *proof kernel* checks derivations against explicit inference rules and
premises. It accepts or rejects the claimed judgment; it does not perform the
application's runtime transitions or supply runtime authorization. It is
separate from the richer elaborator that proposes terms and evidence. It must
not trust the elaborator's conclusion merely because the elaborator supplied it.

A *dedicated semantic checker* establishes a particular judgment or property,
such as exact finite-state exploration or structural application closure,
through its declared checking mechanism. It need not be implemented as part of
the proof kernel. Its result retains the evidence class and trust premises of
that mechanism.

These components may share canonical semantic definitions. Sharing definitions
neither merges their responsibilities nor establishes implementation soundness.
The relationship between the checked subject and the production object remains
an explicit correspondence obligation. Canonical terminology calls the runtime
component the executable semantic core, reserving =proof kernel= for derivation
checking; external proof-assistant kernels must be identified as external.

The trust base of a claim includes every component that establishes a judgment
without supplying evidence checked by another identified mechanism. Depending
on the claim, this includes parsing, typing, normalization, substitution,
transition interpretation, frame rules, initiality, invariant preservation,
induction, certificate admission, and source-to-artifact binding.

A small propositional kernel does not make the whole assurance system equally
small if other modules directly establish additional semantic judgments. Those
modules must either produce evidence accepted by the appropriate checker or
remain explicit parts of the trusted checker implementation. Part IX owns their
rules; Part X owns the corresponding soundness and implementation arguments.

The architecture separates semantic definitions, elaboration, derivation
checking, runtime realization, and observations/reports. Checking a derivation
does not submit a database transaction. An integration runner may perform real
effects to collect evidence, but it is then an effectful realization boundary,
not part of the pure proof kernel. The runtime consumes admitted semantics and
current premises, not a diagnostic report standing in for either.

These definitions fix responsibilities without requiring one monolithic
namespace. Shared definitions reduce drift; independent correspondence checks
and soundness arguments address mistakes shared by execution and checking.

*** Raw declaration

A *raw declaration* is user, application, or framework data that has not yet
crossed the semantic checker appropriate to its role.

Examples include:

- a raw Malli or domain schema;
- a raw business-rule expression;
- a raw operation contract;
- a raw choreography;
- a raw authority declaration;
- a raw derivation;
- a raw dependency reference;
- a raw application assembly declaration.

Raw data is permitted at construction boundaries.

Raw data must not masquerade as a certified semantic artifact merely because it
has the same map shape as one.

*** Admitted semantic value

An *admitted semantic value* is a value that has crossed the construction,
verification, authoritative-acquisition, or other checking boundary required
for the semantic category in which downstream code will consume it.

Admission answers a question such as:

#+begin_quote
May this value be treated by the supported program as an instance of semantic
category C at this boundary?
#+end_quote

Admission does not automatically answer every stronger question about the
value.  In particular:

- admission into a nominal category is not automatically a proof of every
  proposition named by that category;
- admission does not create current authorization unless authorization is the
  proposition established by the trusted runtime boundary;
- admission does not make a client-carried value authoritative merely because
  it was once authoritative before serialization;
- an admitted artifact identity binds the relevant object to its construction
  history but does not establish semantic truth by identity alone.

A nominal type, opaque wrapper, private constructor, checked constructor, or
registry-resolved object may help represent admission.  The requirement is that
unsupported construction or same-shaped raw data cannot silently receive the
stronger semantic treatment through the normal API.

*** Semantic interface

A *semantic interface* is the normalized meaning exported by one checked
component to its dependents.

It contains only propositions downstream reasoning is permitted to rely on.

Depending on the component, this may include:

- declared value types;
- variants;
- cardinality;
- optionality/refinement rules;
- relationships;
- operation input/output facts;
- preconditions;
- postconditions;
- authority requirements;
- provenance requirements;
- effects and resources;
- named assumptions;
- named guarantees;
- compatibility constraints.

An exported interface distinguishes guarantees from the assumptions under
which those guarantees hold. Listing a proposition as a required premise does
not export that proposition as already established. A dependent component must
either inherit the exact applicable premise, discharge it using a checked
justification, or decline the stronger claim. The interface's named obligations
and dependencies must be resolved to their source-owned subjects; a convenient
summary is not an independent source of semantic truth.

Implementation details irrelevant to downstream reasoning should not become
semantic commitments merely because they are convenient to serialize.

*** Assumption

An *assumption* is a proposition accepted from outside the proof currently
being established. It is identified by its proposition *and applicability*:
subject, parameters and bindings, semantic or authority context, scope, origin,
and the implementation/runtime boundary where relevant. Its human-readable
name alone is not sufficient identity. Two claims about current Membership for
different principals, Organizations, Requests, or invocations do not share one
premise merely because both are called =:current-membership=.

Assumptions are explicit leaves in the selected proof justification or
explanation graph. Acceptance under an admission policy keeps the dependent
claim conditional; it does not discharge the assumption. Discharge requires a
separately checked, scope-compatible justification that inherits its own
premises, as specified in Part IX.

They are not inferred from trust in a namespace name, source location, familiar
implementation, passing test, or matching hash.

*** Guarantee

A *guarantee* is a proposition established by the current checked component
relative to its certified dependencies and named assumptions.

*** Obligation

An *obligation* is a proposition required for a particular judgment, admission,
or assurance claim. A mandatory check must be established before its result is
issued. Where admission policy permits an unproved contract as a premise, the
resulting certificate states the dependent conditional judgment; it does not
claim that the original contract obligation was discharged.

An unresolved obligation must remain visible.

It must not be replaced by a default success value or silently omitted from the
result.

*** Derivation

A *derivation* is machine-readable proposed evidence describing how a guarantee
follows from specified premises and inference rules. A raw derivation is not
self-validating. Only a successful check under the applicable calculus and
subject/premise bindings can establish the judgment it proposes; the
interpretation and soundness of that checking mechanism remain separately
classified claims.

Derivations are useful for:

- mechanical checking;
- diagnostics;
- explanation;
- repair;
- trusted-computing-base reduction through independently checked evidence.

A derivation is evidence, not runtime authority.

*** Certificate

A *certificate* is a closed evidence artifact stating enough information for an
appropriate checker to decide the claims it represents. Closed means that its
required references and dependencies are accounted for, including named
assumption leaves. It does not mean that those assumptions have been proved or
that future runtime premises already hold.

A certificate may include:

- the semantic object checked;
- its semantic identity;
- certified dependency identities;
- assumptions;
- guarantees;
- proof obligations;
- derivations or checked references to derivations;
- applicable semantic-checker or proof-kernel version;
- result classification.

A certificate is not a boolean.

A certificate is not accepted because an ordinary Clojure map contains a key
such as =:verified? true=. A submitted certificate-shaped value remains raw
evidence until its required checking/re-admission boundary succeeds.

A certificate is accepted only through the checker and issuer boundaries
appropriate to that certificate class. Admission binds the checked subject,
claims, required source-derived obligations, dependency closure, permitted
premises, and applicability. A copied successful report or a matching digest
does not supply that issuer binding. A partial explanation of failed admission
is useful diagnostic material, not a certificate satisfying the missing claim.

*** Semantic identity

A *semantic identity* is a deterministic identity of the semantic material
normalized under a declared encoding and normalization contract. It identifies
that material; it is not a decision procedure for equivalence of arbitrary
Clojure programs.

It is deliberately distinct from:

- source-file checksum;
- file path;
- namespace name;
- build timestamp;
- runtime locator;
- physical artifact location.

Whitespace, comments, local helper names, and implementation details outside
the exported contract need not alter its semantic identity. A claim about the
concrete implementation may nevertheless need a new implementation binding or
new correspondence evidence after such a change.

Changes to relied-upon semantic commitments must invalidate dependent evidence
unless a checked compatibility or implication argument justifies reuse. A
matching contract identity alone does not authorize evidence reuse across
different implementations.

Examples include changes to:

- value types;
- rule contracts;
- authority contracts;
- operation postconditions;
- provenance requirements;
- compatibility rules;
- resource semantics.

Semantic identity identifies normalized semantic material under the declared
normalization and identity contract. A matching untrusted identifier is not
self-authenticating evidence that the corresponding material was checked.

Semantic identity by itself does not prove the truth of that material. Evidence
about a particular generated artifact or implementation must also remain bound
to the concrete object to which that evidence applies. Equality of normalized
contract material does not automatically transfer test
results or physical artifact correspondence from an earlier build to a different
one. For opaque functions, the exported contract, admitted implementation
binding, and evidence applicability are separate identities. Gesso does not
require deciding extensional equivalence of those functions.

*** Proof DAG

A *proof DAG* is the dependency graph from a root checked artifact through all
certified semantic dependencies and named assumptions.

A root may depend on the following semantic layers; actual dependencies form
a DAG, not necessarily one linear chain:

| Semantic layer | Typical exported evidence |
|----------------+----------------------------|
| Schemas, domains, and relationships | Typed interfaces and relationship constraints |
| Business rules | Well-formedness, proved properties, and explicit opaque contracts |
| Model operations | Transition obligations, invariants, and realization premises |
| Choreography | Knowledge, authority, coordination, and resource judgments |
| Projection | The stated global-to-local behavior relation |
| Executable plans | Semantic and artifact identity bindings |
| Whole application | Closure, production enforcement, and independent acceptance evidence |

The proof DAG records the justifications and inherited premises for the
particular claims being established. Its acyclicity and internal completeness
are necessary, but do not establish that all *required* application obligations
were included. The constructor separately derives the mandatory obligation
ledger and required dependency edges from the original source, actual semantic
subject, and selected profile. It must check that the selected proof DAG
accounts for those obligations as established, explicitly accepted premises
where policy permits, or unresolved blockers. A caller-supplied graph with a
conveniently omitted guard, operation, authority check, writer, or correspondence
edge cannot certify the reduced subject as the complete application.

Alternative sound justifications may have different dependencies; only the
selected justification's actual premises are inherited by its dependent
claim. No premise can be discarded merely because another claim in a shared
artifact is assumption-free. Required obligation coverage and per-claim
dependency closure are separate checks, not a second author-maintained proof
registry. Part IX owns their normative checking and issuance rules.

A later stage may rely only on propositions actually exported by its certified
dependencies or named as assumptions.

No stage receives credit for a predecessor's intended meaning when that meaning
was never certified.

The certificate dependency DAG must be acyclic. Recursive or fixed-point
semantics may be supported only through a deliberately formalized rule with
checked premises; introducing a dependency cycle does not supply that
justification.

This requirement is distinct from ordinary recursive computation. In
particular, a cycle among Clojure namespace dependencies is a load-time
dependency error, not a mechanism for expressing logical recursion. Any
recursive reasoning admitted by the certification system must therefore be
represented explicitly in its semantics rather than encoded as a cyclic
namespace or certificate dependency.
