** Roles, principals, actors, hosts, and authorities
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-semantic-identities
:END:

These concepts are distinct and must remain distinct throughout declaration,
projection, runtime execution, proof, diagnostics, and application assembly.

*** Role

A *role* is a static position in a choreography.

Examples:

#+begin_example
:helper
:request-authority
:observer
#+end_example

A role describes a position in the distributed protocol.

A role is not automatically:

- a user;
- a browser tab;
- a process;
- a web server;
- an authorization grant.

An actor may realize the projected behavior of a role only through an
admitted binding. That binding is not itself a grant of business authority.
The authenticated principal's permission to exercise that role for a subject
and operation is determined at the applicable trusted boundary; dynamic
business permissions must also be protected where the authoritative operation
incorporates its effects. A browser-selected role label or actor ID cannot
supply that permission.

*** Principal

A *principal* is the identity or entity whose authority is being exercised.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:user user-id]
[:service :humanhelp]
#+end_example

A principal is a semantic/security identity.

A principal is not identical to a browser actor.

One principal may have:

- multiple browser tabs;
- multiple devices;
- multiple protocol executions;
- retries through multiple hosts.

Those actors may hold different knowledge while acting for the same principal.

*** Actor

An *actor* is a concrete participant carrying a local projected machine,
execution state, or knowledge state.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:client client-id]
[:service-instance logical-service-actor-id]
#+end_example

An actor gives semantic identity to a participant beyond one physical callback
or request.

The exact runtime realization may be short-lived.

An actor identity does not require a permanently resident process.

*** Host

A *host* is the physical runtime that happens to execute a step.

Examples:

#+begin_example
[:web-node node-1]
[:web-node node-3]
#+end_example

Hosts are replaceable physical realizations.

A retry may run on another host without changing:

- semantic command;
- principal;
- authority;
- logical operation;
- relevant actor identity, where the protocol preserves it.

A host does not acquire semantic authority merely because it executes authority
code.

*** Authority

An *authority* is the semantic locus entitled to establish some class of
authoritative fact.

An authority is named by an authority identifier such as:

#+begin_example
[:authority :request]
[:authority :request request-id]
#+end_example

The identifier does not itself contain an authoritative fact, nor does it name
a particular database record or server process. It names a locus in the
application's semantic model. Application rules, together with Gesso's
execution rules, determine which facts and transitions that locus is entitled
to establish and under which premises.

For example:

#+begin_example
[:authority :request R]
#+end_example

may govern authoritative facts concerning Request =R=, such as its current
lifecycle state, ownership, or the incorporation of a command affecting it.
The concrete facts may be represented by one or several XTDB documents and may
depend on other authoritative observations, such as Membership or Organization
state. Those storage objects help realize the authority; they are not
themselves its semantic identity.

This distinction permits the physical representation to change without
changing what the application means by:

#+begin_example
[:authority :request R]
#+end_example

For example, the same authority could be realized using a different document
layout, a different database, or a different web node while continuing to
govern the same class of semantic facts.

Authority is therefore distinct from both *storage location* and *execution
location*.

A physical web node may temporarily realize an authoritative operation without
becoming the authority itself.

For a Request operation:

#+begin_example
semantic authority
    [:authority :request R]

first host
    [:web-node A]

retry host
    [:web-node C]
#+end_example

Node =A= and node =C= may each exercise the same semantic authority while
executing different attempts of the operation. Process-local identity or
process-local memory does not determine the authority.

The authority's logical identity can survive host replacement. The realization
must still establish continuity of authoritative state, command records, issuer
bindings, and applicable backend semantics. Merely reusing the authority name
after a database reset or migration does not establish that continuity. Old and
new progression values need an admitted comparison or migration relation before
they can be used together.

Consequently, any fact that must survive retries, failover, or migration
cannot derive its semantic force merely from having been remembered by the
same process. It must instead be recoverable from authoritative state, from
evidence validly issued on behalf of the semantic authority, or from another
mechanism whose semantics are explicitly admitted by the application and
Gesso.


*** Server is not a universal semantic identity

"Server" is normally an implementation location or a role category, not one
unique actor identity.

Multiple web nodes may realize the same logical authority while holding
different process-local state.

The design therefore avoids proofs or runtime assumptions that rely on:

#+begin_example
same server process
= same semantic authority
#+end_example

or:

#+begin_example
different server process
= different semantic authority
#+end_example

Neither equivalence is generally valid.

*** Relationship among role, principal, actor, host, and authority

A useful conceptual example is:

#+begin_example
role
    :helper

principal
    [:user U]

actor
    [:client C]

host of the browser-side actor
    user's browser/device

authoritative role
    :request-authority

semantic authority
    [:authority :request R]

host currently realizing authority
    [:web-node A]
#+end_example

A later retry may change the final line while leaving the semantic operation
otherwise intact.

Verification and diagnostics should identify which category an identity belongs
to instead of overloading one generic "id" field wherever practical.

Authentication establishes the principal binding. The operation's trusted
admission and model policy establish whether that principal may instantiate a
particular role for a particular subject. Actor and host identity provide
correlation or realization information; neither substitutes for those decisions.
Changing a browser tab, transport connection, or physical host must not silently
carry permissions into a different principal or authority scope.

An authentication or principal-context instance may also need identity distinct
from the principal itself. Logout, account switching, credential replacement,
or a declared revocation transition can invalidate pending work even when a
later session represents the same principal. The applicable contract states
which bindings survive and which must be re-established. No universal session
epoch or global counter is required.

Identity correspondence evidence must name its actual issuer and trust
boundary. A typed principal value, a matching command ID, or equality between
caller-supplied maps does not authenticate a request, authorize an actor, or
establish that the recorded semantic arguments were the ones admitted. The
physical request path must derive the principal from trusted authentication,
validate the command's operation, subject and arguments against the admitted
binding, and disclose which authorization and backend premises have actually
been established. Structural identity agreement alone is not a certificate
of authority.


A collaborative editor makes the categories easier to follow when several
change independently. Suppose Alice is permitted to edit document D. The
following is inspection data, not a credential accepted from the browser. The fixture symbols U, D, C1, C2, E19, E20, C77, and C88 stand for
prebound values of their respective identity kinds. The Clojure expressions
illustrate the identity relationships; they are not a standalone application,
a credential, or a checked authorization proof.

#+begin_src clojure
(def editor-session
  {:semantic/role :editor
   :principal [:user U]
   :principal-context :session/alice-1
   :actor [:client C1]
   :actor-host [:browser-context :alice-tab-1]
   :authority [:authority :document D]
   :operation :document/save
   :command C77
   :execution E19
   :transport-request-id :http/204
   :authority-host [:web-node :A]})

(def retry-session
  (assoc editor-session
    :transport-request-id :http/205
    :authority-host [:web-node :C]))

(= (select-keys editor-session
     [:principal :principal-context :actor :authority
      :operation :command :execution])
   (select-keys retry-session
     [:principal :principal-context :actor :authority
      :operation :command :execution]))
;; => true
#+end_src

The operation's retry contract preserves command C77, including its admitted
semantic arguments. The authority host and transport request change. The
browser actor's own host does not thereby become node C, and neither physical
location authenticates the principal.

Alice opens a second tab and forms a separate save intent:

#+begin_src clojure
(def second-tab
  (assoc retry-session
    :actor [:client C2]
    :actor-host [:browser-context :alice-tab-2]
    :command C88
    :execution E20
    :transport-request-id :http/206))

(= (:principal retry-session) (:principal second-tab))
;; => true
(= (:authority retry-session) (:authority second-tab))
;; => true
(= (:actor retry-session) (:actor second-tab))
;; => false
(= (:command retry-session) (:command second-tab))
;; => false
#+end_src

These maps isolate identity relationships; they do not implement the save
operation's argument binding or admission checks. The new intent has its own
command identity. If the second tab instead
reconciles C77, it may retain C77 only through that command's binding and
reconciliation contract. Copying a session map is not a command-transfer or
authorization protocol.

The authoritative server path authenticates each invocation independently of
the inspection fields. The following `auth/current-principal!` and
`document-model/save!` functions denote application-owned contracts, not APIs
that this section claims Gesso already provides:

#+begin_src clojure
(defn invoke-save!
  [{:keys [session] :as ctx}
   {:document/keys [id content] :gesso/keys [command-id]}]
  (let [principal (auth/current-principal! session)]
    (document-model/save!
      (assoc ctx :principal principal)
      {:document-id id
       :content content
       :command-id command-id})))
#+end_src

The admitted route binds this entry to the save operation. The public model
operation validates command binding and current disclosure permission for
replay, and current editing authority for new incorporation. It protects the
relevant state dependencies atomically with a new save and its command record.
An authorization check made earlier in a handler is insufficient if the relevant
permission can change before incorporation.

If the Organization revokes Alice's editing permission, a new save intent is
rejected under the current policy. An already incorporated C77 is handled by
its replay protocol: the recorded result is returned only if current disclosure
policy permits it. Revocation must not cause a successful old save to be
misreported as never having happened, nor must historical success bypass the
new disclosure decision. If outcome evidence is unavailable, the caller's
knowledge may remain unknown.

The principal, actors, and semantic authority can remain the same while the
authorization relation changes. Their continued existence does not imply
continued editing rights. Category-specific diagnostics identify which binding
or permission changed rather than reporting an unexplained generic id mismatch.

*** Identity-sensitive admission and replay counterexamples

The collaborative-editor scenario also has distinct negative cases that a
complete application check must cover. They are not reducible to comparing
identity fields in a handler or to checking that the projected role exists:

- *Revocation before new incorporation.* If Alice's current editing permission
  is revoked before a new save is admitted at the qualified authoritative
  incorporation boundary, the new effect is not admitted. A prior positive
  handler check cannot replace transaction-current protection of a dependency
  that can change concurrently. An unauthorized caller also must not be able
  to create a durable terminal rejection for somebody else's command.
- *Revocation racing with incorporation.* The outcome must follow the
  application's qualified atomicity and conflict semantics for the relevant
  authorization dependencies. A previously incorporated effect is not undone
  by a later revocation; neither a process-local ordering guess nor a browser
  timestamp may decide which authoritative transition won.
- *Previously incorporated C77 after revocation.* Compatible, authorized
  read-only resolution uses C77's original principal, operation, subject and
  semantic-argument binding, with a fresh current disclosure decision. It
  does not rerun the save, and a disclosure denial does not turn historical
  success into a claim that the command never happened.
- *Identity or argument substitution.* C77 cannot be transferred to another
  principal or silently rebound to new content, an operation, or an authority
  by changing browser-supplied fields. Incompatible or unauthorized resolution
  is not permission to disclose the recorded result or its existence.
- *Lost or indeterminate response.* Missing, expired, or inaccessible command
  evidence may leave incorporation unknown. It is not evidence of rejection,
  and neither an alternate host nor a fresh request grants an automatic
  resubmission right.
- *Session or host change.* A second browser tab, account switch, credential
  replacement, or retry through node C requires the applicable current trusted
  bindings. Stable command or authority identity cannot carry permissions
  across principal contexts or database incarnations without the declared
  compatibility and recovery contract.

These are normative acceptance and correspondence obligations, not a claim
that a current Gesso gateway already authenticates principals, command
arguments, session context, or backend causality. Application assembly and
assurance reports must retain any such undischarged premises explicitly.
