* Part III — Choreography and projection
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: part-03-choreography-and-projection
:END:

This part defines choreography as a global semantic specification, the operation
contracts it exposes, and the obligations for deriving role-local behavior. It
uses the evidence classifications of Part I and the identity, admission,
knowledge, and authority model of Part II. Concrete model/FX integration follows
in Part IV; browser realization and whole-application construction follow in
Parts VI and VII.

** What a choreography is

A choreography is a global description of a distributed state machine involving
N roles.

The global choreography is projected into N role-local machines. At runtime,
concrete actors instantiate those roles and execute the local machines
independently.

There does not need to be a second centralized global machine executing beside
them. The global choreography is the specification from which local machines
are verified and projected.

Under the declared entry, runtime, and environmental premises, the interaction
of admitted projected machines must refine behavior allowed by the global
choreography. The refinement claim must identify its supported language subset,
observable behaviors, and treatment of internal or physical steps.

This claim about cooperating projected machines does not assume that an
adversarial browser follows its projection. Trusted-side admission and model
enforcement must still preserve the protected authority boundary for arbitrary
client inputs, as specified in [[#req-trust-boundary][the trust model]].

A choreography may describe:

- local computation;
- communication;
- authoritative establishment and observation requirements;
- environmental events;
- branching;
- authoritative operations;
- provisional projections;
- resource acquisition and release;
- failure paths;
- timeout paths;
- settlement and reconciliation;
- convergence requirements.

A choreography does not imply that all participants change state atomically.
Distributed actors may temporarily hold different knowledge.

The important distinction is between:

#+begin_example
authoritative state
provisional state
actor-local knowledge
#+end_example

and not a false claim that the entire distributed system changes simultaneously.

A choreography also does not imply one persistent runtime machine per connected
client or one persistent server process per conceptual workflow.

The global description may be realized by short-lived projections that interact
through durable authoritative facts.

*** Choreography is specification, not a hidden coordinator

The global choreography is the semantic specification of allowed distributed
behavior.  It is not a requirement that Gesso run one omniscient global state
machine alongside every projected role.

The implementation must therefore preserve two complementary truths:

- the global description is authoritative for what behaviors are admitted;
- projected actors execute locally and independently using only the knowledge,
  resources, and authority available to them.

A runtime architecture that requires every local step to consult a central
choreography interpreter solely to rediscover projected control flow would
defeat an important purpose of projection. This does not prohibit a declared
authoritative operation from consulting its centralized model authority or
checking a trusted runtime premise.  It
would turn the global specification into a distributed lockstep coordinator
rather than a source from which local programs are derived and checked.

Conversely, independently executing local machines do not become semantically
independent programs.  Their combined traces remain constrained by the global
description.

The intended relationship is:

#+begin_example
global choreography
        |
        | verify / analyze / project
        v
role-local executable plans
        |
        | execute independently
        v
distributed concrete behavior
        |
        | must refine
        v
behaviors admitted by the global choreography
#+end_example

The absence of a central runtime machine is therefore not a weakening of the
global specification.  It is the point of a successful projection.

*** Semantic behavior versus physical realization

A choreography describes semantic behavior.  Physical realization is permitted
to vary when that variation does not change the semantic trace.

For example, the semantic statement:

#+begin_example
:request-authority establishes the result of :request/claim
#+end_example

does not require:

#+begin_example
one fixed Aleph process
one fixed HTTP connection
one fixed DOM node
one fixed browser tab
one fixed database connection
#+end_example

The authority may be realized through different physical resources at different
times.  A retry may reach another host.  A fragment may be replaced by a new DOM
node.  A browser may reconnect.  None of these physical substitutions creates
a new semantic authority or silently changes the meaning of the operation.

This end-state design therefore requires every layer that crosses a
replaceable physical boundary to preserve the logical identities needed by the
semantic layer rather than reconstruct them from physical coincidence.

Detailed multi-node routing belongs to the later multi-node realization part of
this design.  The semantic requirement established here is only the stronger
and more durable one:

#+begin_quote
A host may realize a role or authority for an execution, but physical hosting
does not create semantic authority.
#+end_quote

*** Choreography may be partial with respect to the whole application

One choreography need not describe an application's entire lifetime.

A choreography may describe one distributed semantic operation or one coherent
short-lived protocol.  Durable model facts may connect many such protocols over
hours, days, deployments, or browser lifetimes.

This means that the following are both legitimate:

#+begin_example
one choreography
    claim Request R
    -> authoritative outcome
    -> settlement
    -> terminate
#+end_example

and:

#+begin_example
choreography E1
    create approval request
    -> commit :approval/pending
    -> terminate

... later ...

choreography E2
    approve request
    -> commit :approval/approved
    -> terminate
#+end_example

The durable relationship between E1 and E2 lives in authoritative model state
unless the application explicitly adopts a durable workflow facility.

This separation prevents Gesso from smuggling a universal workflow engine into
ordinary Choreo merely because some business processes span human time.

*** Choreography validity and application realizability are distinct questions

A choreography can be internally valid while the surrounding application fails
to realize a required use of it. An intentionally unexposed operation is not
automatically an assembly defect: required exposure follows the declared
realization scope and independent product acceptance.

For example, a verified operation may still be unusable because:

- no view binds it;
- no projected browser plan is packaged;
- the packaged artifact is stale;
- no transport binding reaches the trusted server;
- the server has no corresponding execution capability;
- no authoritative acquisition path can observe the successor state.

Missing required realization edges are whole-application assembly failures.
Omitting an intended user capability can instead leave the remaining assembly
closed while failing behavioral completeness. Those distinctions follow the
[[#req-application-assurance-claims][three application assurance claims]] in Part I.
Neither failure changes the meaning of choreography validity itself.

The distinction is important:

#+begin_example
valid choreography
    !=
complete application realization
#+end_example

and equally:

#+begin_example
HTTP route exists
    !=
valid choreography
#+end_example

The unified design therefore preserves Choreo as a strong semantic layer while
also requiring whole-application checks of the joins between semantic and
physical/application layers, production enforcement of those checks, and
independent capability acceptance. Closure results retain their actual assurance
classification rather than automatically becoming semantic theorems.

*** Construction must fail when the contradiction is already knowable

Choreography construction and verification must reject known contradictions at
the earliest supported boundary possessing enough information to establish them.
Unsupported constructs must be identified explicitly; they must not receive a
successful result by omission from the checker.

Definition-time failures include, for example:

- unknown roles;
- malformed transitions;
- impossible branch references;
- invalid message declarations;
- inconsistent operation metadata;
- use of an undeclared authority;
- a local behavior that requires a fact that the role can never know.

These failures should not be deferred to browser execution merely because a
runtime implementation could eventually notice them.

This does not mean Gesso should pretend to know dynamic domain facts in
advance.  A Request becoming claimed by another user after rendering remains a
runtime/domain event.  Static strictness applies only where the contradiction is
already present in the declared semantic structure.

*** Canonical authoring principle

Application authors should state semantic intent once whenever the system can
derive the rest.

A choreography should not require synchronized copies of the same semantic fact
in:

- operation metadata;
- view metadata;
- browser-plan names;
- transport declarations;
- route strings;
- server registries;
- bootstrap registries.

Where an independent physical choice genuinely exists, it may be declared
separately and checked for correspondence.  Otherwise the preferred direction
is:

#+begin_example
one semantic declaration
    |
    +--> derived local plan
    +--> derived binding metadata
    +--> derived/validated transport requirements
    +--> derived diagnostics
#+end_example

This principle does not collapse all layers into one macro.  It reduces
duplicated sources of semantic truth while preserving explicit architectural
boundaries.

** Choreographic operation types
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-choreographic-operation-contract
:END:

A choreographic operation type is richer than an ordinary function type.

An ordinary function type is roughly:

#+begin_example
A -> B
#+end_example

A choreographic operation type describes an entry into a distributed protocol
and the valid continuations from that entry.

It may include:

- ordinary value schemas;
- evolving values and their basis;
- roles participating in the operation;
- principal and authority requirements;
- facts known by each role on entry;
- provenance justifying those facts;
- facts on which roles agree;
- resources required by operations;
- facts and values produced;
- possible outcomes;
- provisional execution permissions;
- authority transitions;
- continuation requirements;
- compatibility/version requirements where a suspended projection may outlive a
  deployment.

The operation type should *not* duplicate the complete choreography by manually
listing every branch already represented by the choreography.

The choreography is the source of truth for control flow.

The type declaration supplies external semantic commitments that cannot or
should not be inferred, while analysis derives as much as possible from the
actual choreography.

For example, the declaration may state:

#+begin_src clojure
{::choreo/roles
 {:helper
  {:principal :user
   :actor :client}

  :request-authority
  {:authority :request}}

 ::choreo/inputs
 {:request-id
  {:schema :uuid
   :known-by #{:helper}
   :provenance :input}

  :observed-basis
  {:schema ObservedBasisClaim
   :known-by #{:helper}
   :provenance :input}}

 ::choreo/authority
 {:request-authority #{:request/claim}}

 ::choreo/optimism
 {:role :helper
  :projection :request/claim}}
#+end_src

This example declares role requirements, input knowledge, and the intended
optimistic realization. It does not grant claim authority to whoever sends a
matching role label. Trusted server admission must establish the concrete
principal, subject, and operation premises. The helper's input knowledge of an
=ObservedBasisClaim= is knowledge of the claimed token, not evidence that the
helper acquired the observation it names.

The names =ObservedBasisClaim= and =AuthoritativeBasis= are conceptual semantic
categories.  They do not require two unrelated physical token formats, nor do
they require a particular Clojure nominal-type implementation.  The browser may
carry the same underlying XTDB token bytes that trusted code later compares or
uses to construct a consistency requirement, while the semantic status of those
values remains different.

XTDB-specific representation belongs in the XTDB/Live consistency adapter rather
than being permanently baked into the generic Choreo type vocabulary.

The verifier derives knowledge changes, branch requirements, and continuation
structure from the choreography body.

The exact Clojure syntax remains tentative. These semantics are the intended
contract.

*** Operation identity is semantic, not transport identity

A choreographic operation has a stable semantic identity that is distinct from
the mechanism used to invoke it.

For example:

#+begin_example
:request/claim
#+end_example

is not synonymous with:

#+begin_example
POST /requests/123/claim
#+end_example

nor with:

#+begin_example
button id="claim-request"
#+end_example

nor with:

#+begin_example
browser plan index 17
#+end_example

The transport, view, and executable-plan coordinates are realizations of the
semantic operation.  They may change across refactors or deployments without
changing the operation's meaning, provided compatibility rules permit the
change.

This distinction is required so that application closure can later verify
correspondence instead of mistaking physical equality for semantic identity.

A stable operation name is also distinct from the normalized semantic identity
of its contract. Keeping =:request/claim= unchanged while changing a relied-upon
authority rule, input requirement, outcome, or postcondition does not preserve
dependent validity merely because the name stayed the same. Conversely, a
transport refactor need not change the semantic contract if its checked
realization and compatibility obligations remain satisfied.

*** Semantic categories at operation boundaries

Operation APIs must preserve distinctions whose accidental substitution would
violate a semantic invariant even when the underlying Clojure representations
are identical or structurally compatible.

Representative distinctions include:

- =ObservedBasisClaim= versus trusted =AuthoritativeBasis=;
- semantic =CommandId= versus runtime =ExecutionId=;
- provisional versus authoritative observations;
- raw declarations versus checker-admitted declarations;
- raw certificate data versus checker-accepted certificate evidence.

The requirement is semantic non-interchangeability through the supported API.
The design does not mandate that every such category be implemented as a
=defrecord=, Java class, wrapper object, or other nominal runtime type.  Permitted
realizations include:

- nominal or opaque host-language values;
- smart constructors with inaccessible or conventionally private construction;
- checker-produced admitted values;
- explicit tagged data whose use is guarded by the appropriate admission path;
- an existing Gesso representation that already prevents the accidental
  substitution.

The choice should be the smallest mechanism that makes the wrong ordinary call
difficult and the resulting diagnostic precise.  A representation technique is
not valuable merely because it looks more typed.

Ordinary downstream application code should normally author domain values and
semantic declarations, not manually wrap every UUID, basis token, command, or
projection in framework ceremony.  Gesso should perform category establishment
at elaboration, checking, authoritative acquisition, model, or runtime
boundaries wherever it can derive the distinction itself.

For example, a useful public failure is conceptually:

#+begin_example
boundary:                trusted claim preparation requiring an acquired basis
requires:                AuthoritativeBasis for the declared scope
received:                unadmitted ObservedBasisClaim
missing step:            trusted authoritative acquisition / admission
#+end_example

rather than accepting the same-shaped value and relying on a comment to explain
why the caller should not have done so. The diagnostic applies at the boundary
that requires the acquired basis. A command intake may legitimately accept an
=ObservedBasisClaim= as weaker context; it must not demand that the browser
manufacture server-trusted authority before submitting a command.

The category and admission rules are defined in
[[#req-semantic-admission][Part II]]. When the supplied category cannot be
established, diagnostics report an unknown or unadmitted value rather than
trusting a client-supplied category label.

A nominal host-language value also does not retain trusted status merely because
it was serialized and reconstructed.  Crossing an adversarial wire boundary
returns the value to an untrusted representation until the relevant verifier,
authenticator, decoder, or trusted acquisition path re-establishes the stronger
semantic category.

This is primarily an accidental-misuse boundary inside cooperating application
code.  It does not claim that deliberately hostile trusted host code is unable
to bypass Gesso, import lower-level implementation namespaces, or directly use a
database capability it already possesses.

*** Operation contracts export a semantic interface

An operation type should expose a normalized semantic interface containing only
facts that downstream reasoning is allowed to rely on.

Depending on the operation, that interface may include:

- input value types;
- required entry knowledge;
- required provenance;
- role participation;
- principal/authority constraints;
- model operation identity;
- possible semantic outcomes;
- values/facts produced;
- authority changes;
- resource requirements;
- provisional permissions;
- settlement requirements;
- continuation compatibility;
- named assumptions and guarantees.

It should not include arbitrary implementation details merely because those
details are easy to serialize.

For example, the operation contract may establish:

#+begin_example
operation:
    :request/claim

requires:
    helper knows Request identity
    for the declared optimistic realization:
        helper has acquired the required scoped authoritative Request projection
    trusted server binds authenticated principal
    request authority may invoke public request.core/claim

outcomes:
    :confirmed
    :reconciled
    :rejected
    :already-incorporated
#+end_example

The outcome names above describe authoritative/model semantic dispositions.
They do not imply that every participant can immediately establish which
disposition occurred.  Participant-relative uncertainty and command-specific
reconciliation are specified in the command/failure layer that follows this
section. The supported operation must define which dispositions actually exist,
their payloads, and the predicates or model outcomes that justify them. This
illustrative list does not require every model to implement all four outcomes.
An unknown participant-relative outcome is not silently converted into any of
these authoritative dispositions.

The semantic interface does not encode incidental realization details such as:

#+begin_example
specific Ring handler var
specific Reitit route index
specific DOM node id
specific XTDB Java class
specific browser bundle offset
#+end_example

Those belong to later realization layers unless independently required by
runtime semantics.

*** External commitments versus derived structure

Operation declarations should state only semantic commitments that cannot or
should not be safely inferred.

The verifier/projector should derive:

- reachable continuations;
- behavior-sensitive branch obligations;
- knowledge propagation caused by declared communication;
- terminal paths;
- local plan structure;
- resource obligations that follow from the body.

It should not require the author to restate those facts manually in metadata.

Redundant declarations are dangerous because each copy may remain individually
plausible while disagreeing with the others.

The preferred rule is:

#+begin_quote
Declare external semantic commitments; derive internal control-flow facts from
the choreography body.
#+end_quote

*** Availability is not authorization

An operation may be present in a model or choreography registry without being
currently executable by a particular principal.

Likewise, a prepared or bound operation may be rendered as an affordance without
becoming durable authorization.

These distinctions must remain explicit:

#+begin_example
operation exists
    !=
operation is valid in this choreography state

operation is valid in this choreography state
    !=
current authenticated principal is authorized now

view renders operation capability
    !=
browser possesses authority
#+end_example

The trusted side re-establishes dynamic authorization at execution and protects
the relevant model premises at the authoritative transition boundary where
intervening changes matter. A rendered affordance, a successful earlier check,
and a valid operation contract do not reserve future eligibility.

This separation lets Gesso reason strongly about operation identity and
distributed protocol structure without pretending to statically prove current
business facts that can change between render and commit.

*** Malformed operation type versus wrong business policy

The unified design distinguishes structural/type malformedness from product
correctness.

If a declaration says that =:request/status= is an enum and a rule attempts:

#+begin_src clojure
(< request.status 10)
#+end_src

the expression is malformed relative to its semantic types and should be
rejected.

If the application states:

#+begin_src clojure
(= request.status :open)
#+end_src

when product policy actually intended another valid status test, the expression
may be perfectly well formed while encoding the wrong policy.

Gesso should make the former difficult or impossible to certify.  It cannot
infer the latter merely from syntax.

This distinction becomes more explicit in the later compositional-proof part,
where typed business-rule expressions and certified semantic interfaces are
defined in detail.  It is already a language-design constraint here because
Choreo must not confuse "type checks" with "is the policy humans intended."

*** A valid rule may still be inadmissible at a distributed program point

Intrinsic rule validity is not enough.

For example:

#+begin_example
rule:
    (= request.private-review :approved)

intrinsic type:
    Boolean
    VALID

browser use before browser knows request.private-review:
    INVALID AT THIS PROGRAM POINT
#+end_example

A distributed use of a rule therefore creates obligations beyond ordinary type
checking, including:

- the role knows every required fact;
- required provenance is available;
- trust or authority requirements are satisfied;
- optional values have been refined before use;
- authoritative-basis requirements are met where the rule depends on
  authoritative observation.

This is one of the central reasons Choreo needs a semantic type/knowledge layer
richer than ordinary function typing.

*** Operation declarations should remain inspectable data

The ordinary authoring surface may eventually include macros or helpers, but the
semantic representation after elaboration should remain explicit enough to:

- inspect;
- normalize;
- verify;
- project;
- fingerprint semantically;
- explain on failure;
- compare against dependent declarations.

Convenient syntax must not create an opaque second semantics.

Normalization must preserve the information needed to identify the owning
declaration and explain failed obligations without making source coordinates
part of semantic identity when they are diagnostic-only. The compiler or
elaborator must not synthesize an undeclared trusted assertion simply to make a
knowledge, authority, or continuation obligation pass.

The desired direction is:

#+begin_example
pleasant author syntax
        |
        v
explicit normalized semantic data
        |
        +--> verification
        +--> projection
        +--> diagnostics
        +--> later certification
#+end_example

** Malli as the value-schema substrate
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-value-schema-boundaries
:END:

Malli should provide the ordinary value-shape layer underneath choreographic
types.

A choreographic value may conceptually carry:

#+begin_src clojure
{:schema ClaimCommand
 :known-by #{:helper :request-authority}
 :agreement #{#{:helper :request-authority}}
 :provenance :communicated}
#+end_src

or:

#+begin_src clojure
{:schema RequestProjection
 :known-by #{:helper}
 :provenance :authoritative
 :basis AuthoritativeBasis}
#+end_src

Malli is responsible for questions such as:

- is this a UUID?
- does this map contain the required keys?
- is this settlement one of the allowed values?
- does this intermediate machine context have the promised shape?

Malli is not responsible for questions such as:

- did this value come from a trusted actor rather than merely having a valid
  shape?
- may this helper claim this Request?
- is this Organization currently open?
- does this principal have authority?
- is this stale basis still semantically compatible with the operation?
- did communication actually establish knowledge?
- did an authoritative observation justify the actor's knowledge?
- does this branch satisfy the global choreography?

Those remain domain, FX, Choreo, or authority/progression concerns.

Runtime validators should be compiled and reused for the admitted schema and
registry generation to which they belong. A relevant schema or registry change
requires an appropriately recompiled or independently validated compatible
validator; an old compiled validator must not silently enforce an obsolete
contract.

The default design should validate important runtime boundaries in production,
including:

- externally supplied choreography inputs;
- message payloads;
- authoritative observation/projection envelopes where they cross a runtime
  boundary;
- FX input/output boundaries;
- projected-machine state transitions;
- settlements;
- provisional/canonical protocol payloads.

The successful path should use compiled boolean validators. Rich Malli
explanations should be constructed only on failure.

The default should be to keep these validations enabled in production and
optimize only when measurement shows a real hot path. Measurement alone does
not justify removing enforcement of a required premise. An optimization must
preserve the contract, for example by avoiding redundant checks after a trusted
admission boundary while retaining checks where untrusted or dynamic values
can enter.

*** Shape validation is necessary but not semantic certification

Successful runtime validation establishes conformance to the particular schema
under the validator and predicate contracts being relied upon. It is classified
as runtime enforcement, not automatically as a formal theorem. A custom
predicate's name does not establish an authority or provenance claim that its
implementation never checks.

It does not prove:

- that a browser supplied the value honestly;
- that the value has authoritative provenance;
- that the current principal may use it;
- that another actor knows the same value;
- that a model operation may be invoked;
- that a choreography branch is globally legal.

This is not a limitation to work around.  It is a deliberate separation of
correctness scopes.

The unified design therefore treats ordinary value schemas as one layer in a
larger semantic interface:

#+begin_example
value shape
    |
    v
semantic type / refinement
    |
    v
knowledge + provenance
    |
    v
authority + operation contract
    |
    v
choreographic admissibility
#+end_example

A success at one layer cannot be silently promoted into a stronger proposition
owned by a later layer.

The same rule applies when a stronger semantic category happens to use a nominal
host-language representation.  Malli may validate the fields inside an
=AuthoritativeBasis= representation, for example, but shape validation does not
establish that the value came through the trusted authoritative acquisition
path.  Conversely, an admitted value may still use ordinary immutable Clojure
data internally.  What matters is the checked construction/admission relation,
not whether the physical carrier is a record or a map.

A discriminator such as =:type :verified= is therefore useful only as data unless
the supported API also establishes why callers are entitled to rely on that
category.

*** Closed value boundaries are the default

Where data crosses a semantic boundary, the declared schema should normally
describe a closed payload.

Closedness is especially important for:

- client commands;
- choreography messages;
- provisional-state envelopes;
- settlement envelopes;
- authoritative projection metadata;
- FX inputs and outputs.

An open map is not harmless merely because consumers ignore unknown keys.
Unexpected fields can become an accidental covert semantic channel when later
code begins to read them.

When an application genuinely needs an open payload, that must be explicit and
its semantic treatment visible to verification and diagnostics. Transporting
opaque extension data need not grant it first-class knowledge status. If a
program wishes to branch on an extension field or consume it as authority,
correlation, or an operation input, that use requires a declared contract and
the appropriate admission.

Semantic payload closure is distinct from rejecting every byte of physical
transport metadata. The decoder may separate declared payload from transport
metadata through an explicit contract. It must reject undeclared semantic
fields at a closed boundary or keep explicitly permitted nonsemantic data
outside the semantic context; it must not silently merge it into that context.

*** Runtime validation preserves static premises

Static reasoning depends on runtime values continuing to satisfy the contracts
under which the reasoning was performed.

Consequently, runtime checks are not "extra tests" after proof.  They are
enforcement of proof premises where untrusted or dynamic values enter.

Representative boundaries include:

#+begin_example
browser bytes
    -> command schema validation
    -> trusted operation lookup

external read result
    -> projection-envelope validation
    -> trusted acquisition / scope / provenance checks
    -> justified knowledge establishment

FX implementation result
    -> declared output validation
    -> next semantic step

settlement payload
    -> settlement schema validation
    -> declared source / command / owner correlation checks
    -> admitted browser transition
#+end_example

If a runtime premise fails, execution must reject or recover according to the
declared protocol.  It must not continue on the theory that static verification
made runtime validation unnecessary.

*** Validators should be reusable and diagnostic failure should be rich

The successful path should use precompiled validators where possible.

The failure path should preserve enough information to explain:

- which semantic value failed;
- which schema or refinement was expected;
- where the value entered;
- which operation or transition depended on it;
- whether failure concerns shape, provenance, authority, or another layer.

A raw Malli explanation may be part of this evidence, but downstream error
reporting should not force users to reconstruct the semantic context from a
low-level schema mismatch alone.

*** Schema changes participate in semantic compatibility

A schema is not merely runtime validation configuration when another semantic
artifact relies upon its meaning.

Changes such as:

- removing an enum member;
- changing required versus optional;
- changing cardinality;
- changing a field's nominal semantic type;
- changing a closed variant set;

may invalidate dependent operation, rule, or choreography artifacts.

Later proof/certificate work therefore assigns semantic identities to normalized
interfaces rather than source files. These operational schema requirements do
not depend on completion of the later certificate machinery. The language/model
boundary must nevertheless expose those semantic dependencies instead of hiding
them inside arbitrary host-language code.

** Projection and branch knowledge
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-projection-branch-knowledge
:END:

Projection creates a local machine for each role.

Projection must preserve whatever mechanism is required to justify local
behavior.

Sometimes that mechanism is communication.

Sometimes the actor already has justified entry knowledge.

Sometimes the relevant fact is learned from authority outside the current
execution and enters a later choreography as authoritative input knowledge.

The projector/verifier must therefore reason in terms of knowledge requirements,
not merely message presence.

A role may branch only when it knows enough information to choose the branch.

The verifier should use behavior-sensitive choice analysis:

- if a role behaves differently between two branches, it must know the
  discriminator or an equivalent fact before its behavior diverges;
- the required knowledge may be justified through communication, authoritative
  observation, input knowledge, declared derivation, or explicit assertion;
- if its behavior is equivalent across the branches, it does not need to learn
  the choice merely because it is syntactically mentioned.

This is intentionally more precise than a simple "every role mentioned in either
branch must know the condition" rule.

For example:

#+begin_example
authority chooses approved/rejected

helper behavior differs afterward
        |
helper must know enough to distinguish the continuations
#+end_example

That requirement does not imply a dedicated authority-to-helper choreography
message if the helper can instead obtain the required approval fact through a
declared, authorized authoritative observation that satisfies the same branch
obligation.

The substitution must preserve subject, scope, provenance, and the relationship
to the decision being continued. Reading a currently approved Request does not
necessarily establish which earlier command was approved or which historical
branch was selected. If later model changes can erase or supersede the needed
fact, the protocol needs a suitable retained outcome, receipt, communication,
or different continuation contract. The projector must not invent that history
or treat an arbitrary later read as equivalent evidence.

Messages should be closed by default.

A message contract declares the fields that may cross the boundary. Arbitrary
extra fields should not be merged into choreography context.

An explicit =:open-payload? true= may exist for cases that genuinely require an
open payload, but closed contracts are the default.

Within the admitted choreography semantics, a sender must have justified
knowledge of every semantic fact it transmits. Receipt from an adversarial
participant still requires the trusted-boundary treatment defined in
[[#req-knowledge-provenance][the knowledge model]].

Receiving a message should only introduce the declared communicated values into
the receiving context.

Projection must not erase communication that is genuinely required to justify
knowledge.

Conversely, projection should not manufacture communication merely because the
global choreography contains a choice when the target role's behavior does not
depend on it or its knowledge is established through another valid path.

*** Projection is a semantic compilation boundary

Projection is not a convenience transformation that happens to produce smaller
maps.

It is the boundary at which the global specification is turned into the local
information and actions available to a role.

A correct projector must therefore preserve every fact required to justify
local behavior, including when relevant:

- message schemas;
- correlation identity;
- entry knowledge;
- knowledge-producing communication;
- authoritative-observation requirements;
- branch discriminators;
- provenance requirements;
- resource ownership;
- failure/terminal structure;
- executable guards required by local semantics.

At the same time, it should omit global facts that the role neither knows nor
needs.

The target is neither "copy the whole global machine to everyone" nor "erase
everything inconvenient."  It is a justified local program.

*** Projection cannot create knowledge

A projector may rearrange or encode information justified by the global
choreography.  It may not manufacture a fact merely because the local machine
would be easier to implement if the fact were present.

Bad:

#+begin_example
global:
    only authority knows :approved?

project browser:
    include :approved? in browser context anyway
#+end_example

Good:

#+begin_example
global:
    authority communicates approved outcome
or
    browser performs authorized authoritative observation

project browser:
    local branch becomes possible after that knowledge path
#+end_example

The same principle applies to provenance.  Projected data cannot silently
upgrade:

#+begin_example
asserted -> derived
communicated -> authoritative
client supplied -> trusted principal identity
#+end_example

Projection preserves or weakens claims according to explicit semantics; it does
not strengthen them by serialization.

*** Behavior-sensitive choice is the central branch rule

A role needs to know a choice only when its observable local behavior depends on
that choice. The required knowledge must exist before the first differing
behavior, not merely become available eventually.

This avoids both extremes:

- projecting too little and leaving a role unable to decide its next behavior;
- overcommunicating every global discriminator to every mentioned role.

The analysis should compare continuations semantically rather than merely by
source syntax.

If two global branches project to equivalent local behavior for role R, R need
not learn the branch solely because R appears somewhere inside both branches.

If they diverge, the projector/verifier must establish a valid knowledge path
before the divergence.

**** Supported equivalence and conservative rejection

Behavior-sensitive checking must define the local equivalence relation used by
its supported language subset. The observables include whichever messages,
effects, authoritative invocations, resource actions, and terminal or failure
outcomes the semantic contract distinguishes. Relevant ordering and timing
constraints must not disappear merely because the final values happen to match.

Permitted internal steps may be abstracted only under an explicit semantic
relation. The checker is not required to decide equivalence of arbitrary
Clojure functions or unrestricted programs.

When the supported analysis cannot establish equivalence or a sufficient
knowledge path, it must reject or report an unresolved obligation at the
appropriate admission boundary. It must not silently assume equivalence,
disclose a private discriminator, or introduce an undeclared message to make
projection succeed. An explicit supported communication path or visible trusted
assumption may be authored where permitted by the relevant contract.

**** Trace preservation and progress are separate obligations

Excluding forbidden local traces is a safety claim. It does not by itself prove
that a projected program can perform every required interaction, avoid deadlock,
or eventually complete. A projection that admits no useful behavior must not
satisfy the required operation merely because its empty behavior is safe.

Each claimed projection result must state the global/local behavior relation
and the entry and environmental premises under which it applies. Required
realizability, terminal behavior, and progress obligations must be discharged
with the appropriate evidence. Liveness remains conditional on its named
delivery, availability, fairness, or application-action assumptions.

These obligations supplement, rather than replace, the independent application
capability acceptance defined in Part I.

*** Message payload closure protects knowledge boundaries

Closed messages are not only a schema preference.  They are a knowledge-flow
boundary.

If a message declares:

#+begin_src clojure
{:request-id UUID
 :outcome ClaimOutcome}
#+end_src

then receiving it may establish only the declared communicated facts.

The receiver does not acquire arbitrary sender context, and the implementation
must not merge unknown payload keys into machine context by convenience.

An explicitly open payload must follow the extension-data contract from the
[[#req-value-schema-boundaries][value-schema boundary]]. Openness alone does not
grant undeclared fields semantic knowledge status or permission to influence a
branch. Any weaker guarantee actually selected remains visible in the result.

*** Projection preserves necessary communication but does not worship messages

Communication is one way to establish knowledge, not the definition of
knowledge.

A projector must preserve a message when that message is what justifies another
role's behavior.

It must not insert a redundant direct message merely because one actor's choice
eventually affects another actor if the other actor can instead obtain the
necessary fact by:

- justified entry knowledge;
- authoritative observation;
- deterministic derivation;
- another declared knowledge path.

This matters deeply for Gesso's web architecture because durable business facts
often rendezvous naturally through model authority and reread rather than
long-lived direct participant messaging.

*** Local executable guards belong in the executable projection

Proof derivations and global proof trees do not belong in the production
browser.

However, a local predicate or guard whose result is required to execute the
projected semantics is runtime-semantic data and must remain in the executable
projection.

The distinction is:

#+begin_example
"browser may branch only if generation is current"
    -> executable guard if needed at runtime

"here is the proof tree explaining why that guard is sufficient"
    -> diagnostic/proof artifact
#+end_example

The production machine must never depend on an optional proof sidecar to decide
a transition.

*** Projection failure should identify the missing justification

When projection cannot produce a legal local machine, diagnostics should explain
the actual semantic deficit.

Useful failure information includes:

- role;
- global state/transition;
- local behaviors that diverge;
- required discriminator;
- facts currently known;
- available provenance;
- missing knowledge path;
- candidate communication or authoritative-observation paths when derivable.

A generic "projection failed" error throws away precisely the information that
the verifier already computed.

*** Host-language callbacks may not bypass projected semantics

A local runtime may invoke host-language functions to realize declared local
effects, but arbitrary callbacks must not become an alternate transition path.

In particular:

#+begin_example
projected machine
    -> declared local effect
    -> host implementation
    -> normalized completion/failure
    -> projected machine
#+end_example

is compatible with the design.

But:

#+begin_example
host callback
    -> mutate choreography state directly
#+end_example

reopens the proof and knowledge boundary.

The common boundary is
[[#req-owner-relative-event-admission][owner-relative event admission]] in Part II.
Part VI develops its browser implementation. An execution-private effect
completion must not bypass that boundary, and a declared shared event must use
its actual fragment or subscription owner rather than an invented execution.

** Long logical workflows and short runtime executions
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: req-durable-workflow-boundary
:END:

A choreography may describe a logical workflow that spans human-scale time.

That does not imply that one Aleph node should retain a suspended server-side
machine in process memory for hours or days.

For example, a business flow may conceptually involve:

#+begin_example
request approval
        |
wait
        |
approval granted
        |
continue
#+end_example

A robust HumanHelp realization may instead be:

#+begin_example
short choreography E1
    |
commit authoritative :approval/pending
    |
E1 terminates

...minutes or hours...

approver observes pending authority
    |
short choreography E2
    |
commit authoritative :approval/approved
    |
E2 terminates

requestor later observes approved authority
    |
short choreography E3 if further action is needed
#+end_example

The durable wait lives in model state.

Subject to the authoritative store's declared durability and recovery
assumptions, the durable workflow fact can survive:

- web-node failure;
- deployment;
- load-balancer rerouting;
- client disconnect;
- browser suspension;
- long offline periods.

The initial Gesso choreography runtime therefore does not promise durable
persistence of arbitrary suspended server-local executions.

If a future application genuinely needs durable workflow-machine continuations,
that should be an explicit opt-in capability with its own persistence, ownership,
versioning, and recovery semantics.

It must not silently become a requirement of ordinary choreography.

*** Durable waiting belongs in authoritative state by default

Human-scale waiting is normally represented as durable domain state rather than
a suspended in-memory choreography execution.

This has several consequences.

First, subject to those persistence and compatibility assumptions, the durable
workflow meaning can survive:

- deployment;
- process loss;
- browser suspension;
- load-balancer rerouting;
- reconnect;
- long periods without any participant online.

Second, later actors can acquire the relevant facts by authoritative
observation rather than requiring the original process to resume.

Third, the framework avoids making one runtime's memory an accidental source of
business truth.

*** A later choreography is not automatically the same execution

When durable state connects two periods of activity, the later protocol may be
a new execution with a new execution identity.

For example:

#+begin_example
E1:
    submit approval request
    -> command C1
    -> commit :pending
    -> terminate

E2:
    approver later acts
    -> command C2
    -> commit :approved
    -> terminate
#+end_example

The business process is continuous in domain meaning, but E1 and E2 need not be
one persistent protocol execution. E2 must establish its own authenticated
principal, current model eligibility, required knowledge, and applicable plan.
The existence of E1's durable result does not carry E1's transient authority or
resource ownership into E2.

This distinction avoids conflating:

- semantic command identity;
- protocol execution identity;
- durable domain identity;
- workflow/business identity.

Where an application truly requires a durable suspended execution, that feature
must define its own:

- persistence;
- ownership;
- versioning;
- migration;
- replay;
- resource cleanup;
- liveness;
- recovery semantics.

It is not silently inherited from ordinary Choreo.

*** Durable model state is the normal rendezvous point

For long workflows, authoritative state provides a stable rendezvous point
between actors who were never concurrently connected.

A later actor may know what happened because authority establishes:

#+begin_example
:approval/pending
:approval/approved
:request/claimed
:request/completed
#+end_example

not because it receives a packet from the actor who caused that state.

This follows [[#req-authoritative-observation][Part II's authoritative observation
model]]: observation is a first-class knowledge path.

Durable pending state is not itself a progress guarantee. The application must
provide the declared opportunity for an eligible actor to discover and act on
it, or state the environmental premises on which progress depends. Where loss
of a required participant can strand the workflow, the runtime-condition and
recovery requirements from Part I apply. Any lease, reassignment, cancellation,
or other successor policy remains model-owned.

*** Deployment compatibility remains explicit

A durable business fact may outlive the executable plan that originally
produced it.

That is normal.

What cannot be assumed is that a suspended *runtime execution* from an older
deployment remains compatible forever.

The later stale-plan/recovery design therefore treats durable model truth and
runtime-plan compatibility as separate dimensions.

A deployment may invalidate continuation of an old projection while leaving the
authoritative business state entirely valid. If a deployment also changes the
meaning or representation of persisted domain state, the model needs its own
compatible interpretation or migration. Restarting a current browser plan does
not perform that domain migration by implication.
